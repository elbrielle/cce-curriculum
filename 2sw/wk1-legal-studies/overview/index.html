    Week 1: Order in the Court — Legal Careers

    2nd Six Weeks | Law, Public Safety, Corrections & Security Cluster | 5 class periods (50 min each)

    Lesson Objective

    Students explore the Law, Public Safety, Corrections & Security career cluster through the Hats & Ladders app and the Find Your Future workbook, investigate legal careers and their education pathways, design a 10-item emergency kit in "Emergency Essentials: Kit Design," draft and revise town ordinances as a council member in "City Council in Action," and use the "Policy Showdown" legal review protocol to argue a position on artificial intelligence in the courts. Students connect legal careers to Irving ISD's Legal Studies program of study at Singley Academy.

    Learning Target: I can name at least three legal careers and the training each one needs, design an emergency kit and defend my choices, write two town laws that are clear, fair, and realistic, and argue a position on AI in the courts using evidence.

    Success Criteria: I will know I am successful when my career research worksheet lists the job, the training, the pay, and the tools for one legal career, when my emergency kit shows 10 labeled items with a reason for my top 3, when both of my town laws answer what the law is, why it is needed, and what happens if someone breaks it, and when my position paper names one legal career and one professional association affected by AI in the courts.

    Demonstration of Learning

    "I can describe at least three careers in the legal field, explain the education pathway from middle school to becoming a lawyer or paralegal, design an emergency kit using critical thinking, draft a town law that is clear, fair, and realistic, and argue a position on AI in the legal system using evidence."

    TEKS Alignment

    • d(1)(B): Explore and describe the CTE career clusters (Day 1).
    • d(1)(C): Identify various career opportunities within one or more career clusters (Days 1-3, 5).
    • d(3)(I): Define entrepreneurship and identify entrepreneurial opportunities within a field of personal interest (Day 4 Legal Entrepreneur Card).
    • d(3)(H): Identify professional associations affiliated with a particular career pathway (Days 4-5).

    Materials Needed

    • Chromebooks with internet access (1 per student)
    • Hats & Ladders student accounts + Find Your Future workbook, Law and Public Safety chapter (printed pp. 39-58)
    • Printed legal career research worksheet (CCE artifact; the research format is taught in Wk0)
    • Printed Emergency Kit Design worksheet (10-item planning grid) or blank paper
    • Blank paper or chart paper for the Day 3 town plan and law drafts (FYF pp. 41-42: "City Council in Action")
    • Printed argument sheets for the Day 4 legal review (FYF p. 46: "Policy Showdown")
    • Printed Legal Entrepreneur Card template
    • BLS, Lawyers: bls.gov/ooh/legal/lawyers.htm
    • BLS, Paralegals: bls.gov/ooh/legal/paralegals-and-legal-assistants.htm
    • iCivics free games (Day 3 extension and support option): icivics.org
    • eDynamic Learning Unit 5.1: Law & Order
    • Xello accounts (Life Experience activity)
    • Projector for video and discussion

    Career Connection

    The legal field includes courtroom attorneys, paralegals who prepare case documents, mediators, consultants, and attorneys who run solo practices. The Law and Public Safety cluster reaches beyond the courtroom to city council members who write local law, firefighters and paramedics who respond to emergencies, and wardens who enforce environmental rules. That range is why this week includes both a law-drafting activity and an emergency kit design.

    What is Happening at Irving ISD? The workbook's district pages (FYF pp. 56-57: "What is Happening at My District?") name two Irving ISD programs of study for this cluster. Legal Studies builds legal research, legal writing, rules of procedure, case management, law office technology, and legal ethics, with concentrations in criminal, family, and workplace law. Criminal Justice builds criminal law, investigation, and appropriate legal response to violations, and covers the history, organization, and functions of local, state, and federal law enforcement. Students in the Legal Studies program at Singley Academy work in a replica courtroom and run mock trials as defense attorneys, prosecutors, and judges. The related industry certification is Non-Commissioned Security Officer Level 2, and the student organizations are Explorers and Mock Trial. The district pages also introduce Dual Credit, which lets a high school student earn college credit in the same course. Two local dual credit options are the Paralegal Associate of Applied Science and the Junior Legal Assistant Certificate, both at Singley Academy.

    Vocabulary

    • Paralegal: A professional who assists lawyers with legal research, document preparation, and case management. Does not require a law degree.
    • Probable Cause: A reasonable basis to believe a crime may have been committed, allowing legal action like an arrest or search.
    • Due Process: The legal requirement that the government must respect all legal rights owed to a person.
    • Bar Association: A professional association for lawyers. Most states require lawyers to pass the Bar Exam to practice law.
    • Entrepreneur (Legal): A person who starts their own legal business, solo practice, mediation firm, or legal consulting.
    • Emergency Kit: A pre-packed set of essential supplies designed to help people survive a disaster or emergency for 72 hours.
    • Ordinance: A local law passed by a city or town council. A strong ordinance is clear, fair, and realistic enough to enforce (FYF p. 40).
    • Policy: A written rule an organization or a city adopts to solve a problem. Legal review teams argue for and against a policy before it is approved (FYF p. 44).

    Bridge to Theory (Hats & Ladders)

    The Find Your Future workbook gives Law and Public Safety printed pages 39-58. The chapter opener (FYF p. 39: "Exploring the World of LAW AND PUBLIC SAFETY") frames the cluster as fast action paired with careful decision-making: some roles respond in the moment during emergencies, while others investigate, analyze, and piece together what happened. Three Hats appear on the opener page, Judge, Firefighter, and Fish and Game Warden, above a "Be the Decision Maker" prompt that puts students in a city safety inspector's shoes on the opening night of a music festival. The chapter prints no pathway list. Students meet the pathways in the Hats & Ladders app through the Pathway Possibilities step on the App Exploration page (FYF p. 58: "App Exploration"), and they meet Irving ISD's own programs of study on the district pages (FYF pp. 56-57).

    This week uses three chapter activities:

    • Emergency Essentials: Kit Design (Day 2): Career Climb activity. Students are a firefighter building a kit citizens can grab during an emergency. They choose an earthquake, fire, or flood scenario, select exactly 10 items from the workbook's 24-item list, draw and label the kit, and share the design with a partner who chose a different scenario (FYF pp. 50-51: "Emergency Essentials: Kit Design").
    • City Council in Action (Day 3): Career Climb activity. Students are a council member for a brand new town. They design the town, scan it for problems in four areas, draft two ordinances that each answer what the law is, why it is needed, and what happens if someone breaks it, then peer-review a partner's laws and revise one (FYF pp. 40-43: "City Council in Action").
    • Policy Showdown (Day 4): Career Climb activity. Students join a City Legal Review Team, split into a Supporting Lawyer and an Opposing Lawyer, prepare a one to two minute argument built from an opening statement, three pieces of evidence, and a closing statement, then present to a peer pair acting as judges who approve the policy, send it back for revision, or reject it (FYF pp. 44-47: "Policy Showdown").

    The chapter's other two activities, Clinton Lake Case (FYF pp. 48-49) and Injured on the Trail (FYF pp. 52-55), belong to Wk 2 First Responders, which shares this chapter. Preview them on Day 5 rather than running them here. The Law and Public Safety chapter carries no Powerskill lesson.

    IISD Instructional Strategies

    • Think-Pair-Share: Day 1, Activity 1, the "Be the Decision Maker" prompt on the chapter opener (FYF p. 39), where a city safety inspector must let a music festival start, delay it, or shut it down after the main stage begins shaking. Distinguished: pose it to the whole group, hold a silent 30 second Think, then time Partner A for 30 seconds and Partner B for 30 seconds, and post two stems at different levels: "I would _ because " and "Delaying costs the city __, but letting the show go on risks _____." Ask two pairs to share, not one. Listen while pairs talk for who names what information they would go get before deciding, and pick your share-out pairs from what you heard rather than from raised hands. Close by asking who would rather argue the call in front of a crowd and who would rather be the one who checked the paperwork beforehand, which is the first sort students make between courtroom and behind-the-scenes legal work.
    • Stop and Jot: Day 1, Activity 2, the two pauses during the Law and Public Safety cluster tour video. Distinguished: name both stop points before the video starts, give 45 seconds per jot with the prompt on screen, and offer two stems: "One legal or public safety career I had never heard of is _" and "One thing I still want to know about is __." Read jots over shoulders during both pauses and track which careers get named. If most jots say Lawyer only, steer the Hat browse that follows toward Court Reporter, Paralegal, 911 Dispatcher, and Fish and Game Warden so the research worksheets are not four copies of the same career.
    • Active Monitoring: Day 3, Activity 2, the three stages of "City Council in Action." Distinguished: walk a fixed pathway three times, one lap per stage, each lap with a single target (town named with a climate and a way people get around, all four rows of the problem table filled, both laws carrying a what, a why, and a consequence), marking a clipboard roster as you go. Have the feedback ready for the two known misconceptions before class starts: a "law" written as a wish rather than a rule ("everyone should be nice at the skatepark"), and a consequence nobody could actually enforce. If more than a handful of students miss the second target, pivot and model one problem row on the projector for the whole room instead of coaching desk by desk.
    • Chunking: Day 2, Activities 1 through 3, the four steps of "Emergency Essentials: Kit Design." Distinguished: release one step at a time, say what each step is for before releasing it (the scenario decides the kit, the list decides the trade-offs, the drawing proves a stranger could use it, the partner share tests it), and close each chunk with a quick check before the next one opens. Read the 10-item lists after the second chunk. If most kits are stacked with comfort items, re-teach with the workbook's own line that an item helping in one scenario may not be as effective in another, then reopen the list before students start drawing.
    • Time, Voice, Body (TVB): Day 4, Activity 2, the prepare-and-present rotation. Distinguished: post the three blocks before the first pair prepares, Voice 1 for partner prep, Voice 3 for the lawyer presenting, and Voice 0 while the judging pair confers, with a visible 6 minute countdown for preparation, then 90 seconds per presenter and 1 minute per ruling. Post the two position stems from the plan where every pair can see them, and define where the judging pair sits and where finished argument sheets go so transitions do not eat presenting time. Narrate the pairs who are meeting the expectation rather than correcting the ones who are not. If the first rotation runs long, cut the judges to one question each and announce the change before the next pair starts.

    Formative Assessment

    • Legal career research worksheet + Comparison Matrix exit ticket (2 legal careers x education/salary/task). Day 1, d(1)(B), d(1)(C)
    • Emergency Kit design + Ranked Justification exit ticket (top 3 items ranked by criticality). Day 2, d(1)(C)
    • Two drafted ordinances + one revised law + Decision Tree exit ticket (enforcement branch). Day 3, d(1)(C)
    • Prepared legal argument + judges' ruling + SCR position paper draft (position, evidence, affected career, association). Day 4, d(3)(I), d(3)(H)
    • H&L Favorites + Xello Life Experience + Concept Map exit ticket (career + association + Life Experience + Irving ISD program of study). Day 5, d(1)(C), d(3)(H)

    Summative Assessment

    AI Ethics Position Paper (Day 4-5): Students write a 5-7 sentence position paper on whether AI should be used in the legal system. The paper must cite at least one legal career that would be affected, identify one professional association that might weigh in, and reference at least one piece of evidence raised during the Day 4 Policy Showdown arguments or found in the Day 1 legal career research. Scored on argument clarity (d(1)(C)), entrepreneurship awareness (d(3)(I)), and association identification (d(3)(H)).

    Differentiation

    Scaffolded Learning

    • Pre-filled legal career research worksheet with one career (Court Reporter) modeled
    • Emergency Kit worksheet with the 3 workbook scenarios listed and 3 items pre-suggested
    • Town-plan template for Day 3 with the six Design Your Town questions already printed and one problem row filled in as a model
    • Sentence stems for the Day 4 legal review and the position paper

    Extensions

    • Research a famous Supreme Court case and identify the different legal professionals who contributed
    • Build a 4-year plan for the Singley Academy Legal Studies program of study
    • Compare the 10-item Emergency Kit for two different scenarios (earthquake vs. flood): what changes and why?
    • Compare salaries of three legal careers across Texas, California, and New York using BLS state data
    • Run the workbook's own Policy Showdown case as written (FYF pp. 44-47): the city park plastic-waste policy, with its own requirements and enforcement rules
    • Play an iCivics courtroom or county-government game ("Do I Have a Right?", "Argument Wars", "Counties Work") and compare the decisions the game forces to the laws drafted on Day 3

    ELL Language Support

    • Pre-teach: Lawyer = Abogado, Paralegal = Asistente Legal, Judge = Juez, Due Process = Debido Proceso, Ordinance = Ordenanza, Emergency = Emergencia, Kit = Equipo
    • Bilingual emergency kit item list (Flashlight = Linterna, First Aid Kit = Botiquín, Whistle = Silbato)
    • Bilingual law-drafting and argument sentence stems
    • The workbook reads well above grade level, so read the scenario paragraphs aloud on Days 2, 3, and 4 and encourage browser translation for any independent reading
