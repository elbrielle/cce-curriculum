    Week 3: Green Machines — Sustainable Engineering

    3rd Six Weeks | Agriculture, Food & Natural Resources Cluster | 5 class periods (50 min each)

    Lesson Objective

    Students explore environmental and sustainable engineering careers inside the Agriculture, Food, and Natural Resources cluster, complete the Find Your Future "Ag-Tech Pest Patrol" activity (FYF pp. 93-95) where they read three sets of field notes from Green Valley Farms and design a pest-detection drone for a 50-acre Texas tomato farm, analyze how changing societal needs create new careers, and complete the Xello Interests lesson + eDynamic Unit 7.1.

    Learning Target: I can name environmental and ag-tech careers in the Agriculture cluster, turn farm field notes into design rules for a drone, design and label that drone, and explain how a change in what society needs makes a new career grow.

    Success Criteria: I will know I am successful when my notes list the job, the problem it solves, the training, and the pay for two environmental careers, when my field notes summary has a filled row for the Ag Engineer, the Farmer, and the Plant Scientist, when my drone design shows at least 6 labeled parts and each label names the field notes problem it solves, and when my societal trends chart has at least 5 rows with one new career and one changing career in each row.

    Demonstration of Learning

    "I can describe sustainable engineering and environmental careers, explain how societal changes create new career opportunities, design a pest-detection drone with labeled features, and connect environmental challenges to specific job roles in the Ag cluster."

    TEKS Alignment

    • d(1)(A): Analyze and discuss assessment results (Day 5 Xello Interests).
    • d(1)(C): Identify various career opportunities within the Ag cluster (Days 1-5).
    • d(1)(D): Research and evaluate emerging occupations related to career interest areas (Day 4 societal trends chart + Day 5 reflection).
    • d(5)(C): Analyze the effects of changing employment trends, societal needs, and economic conditions on career choices (Day 4).

    Materials Needed

    • Chromebooks with internet access (1 per student)
    • Hats & Ladders student accounts + Find Your Future workbook, Agriculture, Food, and Natural Resources chapter (printed pp. 87-102). This week uses the chapter opener (p. 87), "Ag-Tech Pest Patrol" (pp. 93-95), the district pages (pp. 100-101), and "App Exploration" (p. 102)
    • Find Your Future p. 146: "Powerskill: Adaptability" (Day 5 fallback and extension)
    • Xello student accounts (Interests Lesson)
    • eDynamic Learning Unit 7.1: Progressive and Adaptable
    • Chart paper for drone designs + colored pencils/markers (the workbook designs the drone on chart paper, FYF p. 95)
    • Sticky notes for the Step 2 brainstorm (FYF p. 94)
    • BLS, Environmental Engineers: bls.gov/ooh/architecture-and-engineering/environmental-engineers.htm
    • BLS, Agricultural Engineers: bls.gov/ooh/architecture-and-engineering/agricultural-engineers.htm
    • NASA Climate Kids: climatekids.nasa.gov
    • Printed Field Notes Summary worksheet (CCE artifact). The three sets of field notes themselves are printed in the workbook (FYF pp. 93-94), so no separate packet is needed
    • The peer feedback form is printed in the workbook (FYF p. 95). Print extra copies only if students need a clean sheet for a second reviewer

    Career Connection

    Environmental engineers design systems that clean water and reduce pollution. Agricultural engineers design farm equipment, irrigation systems, and crop-monitoring drones. This week puts those jobs on one 50-acre tomato farm, where students have to turn a farmer's pest problem, a plant scientist's observations, and an engineer's requirements into one drone design.

    What is Happening at Irving ISD? The workbook's district pages for this cluster put Mentorship at the center (FYF pp. 100-101): a mentor gives guidance and advice out of real experience, which is how most ag-tech workers learn equipment that changes every few years. Irving ISD's Agricultural Science program at Nimitz High School runs a greenhouse, an orchard, more than 40 chickens, and a daily petcare program, and its two programs of study are Plant Science and Animal Science. Students there can earn the Elanco Veterinary Medical Applications certification and compete through FFA. Sustainable Engineering (Nimitz HS) leads to AutoDesk CAD and Inventor certifications, the same software professional ag and environmental engineers use to design drones, irrigation systems, and field equipment.

    Vocabulary

    • Environmental Engineer: Uses engineering principles to solve environmental problems like water pollution, waste management, and air quality.
    • Agricultural Engineer: Designs farm machinery, irrigation systems, drones, and food processing equipment.
    • Precision Agriculture: Using technology like GPS, drones, and sensors to optimize farming practices.
    • Thermal and Multispectral Imaging: Cameras that capture heat, or light beyond the visible range, to detect plant health, water stress, and pests. The Ag Engineer's field notes call for thermal or heat sensors on the drone (FYF p. 93).
    • Targeted Spraying: Spraying pesticide only where the pests actually are instead of over the whole field. The Ag Engineer's field notes name it as the better choice for the environment (FYF p. 93), and it is the clearest sustainability argument for the drone students design this week.
    • Engineering Design Process: A repeating cycle: Define → Research → Plan/Design → Build/Prototype → Test → Improve.
    • Societal Need: A changing requirement of society (climate awareness, food security, pollution control) that creates demand for new careers.

    Bridge to Theory (Hats & Ladders)

    The Find Your Future workbook gives Agriculture, Food, and Natural Resources printed pages 87-102. The chapter opener (FYF p. 87: "Exploring the World of AGRICULTURE, FOOD, AND NATURAL RESOURCES") frames the cluster as growing food, caring for animals, and managing the land and water everyone depends on, puts a "Be the Decision Maker" prompt in front of students about designing a future farm that has to feed a busy city, and introduces three Hats on the page: Feedlot Manager, Agricultural Engineer, and Veterinarian. The chapter prints no pathway list. Students meet the Agriculture pathways in the Hats & Ladders app through the Pathway Possibilities step on the App Exploration page (FYF p. 102: "App Exploration") and meet Irving ISD's own programs of study on the district pages (FYF pp. 100-101).

    This week's anchor is Ag-Tech Pest Patrol (FYF pp. 93-95), a four-step activity set at Green Valley Farms, a 50-acre tomato farm losing crops to flea beetles, spider mites, and tomato hornworms:

    • Step 1: Read Field Notes (pp. 93-94). Three sets, from Rowan Lindstrum the Ag Engineer, Dale Jimenez the Farmer, and Evelyn Carter the Plant Scientist. The workbook has the class read them together, circle or highlight key information, and discuss what each professional adds to the design. The Ag Engineer's set is organized as Farm Overview, Pest Challenges, and Drone Design Needs; the Farmer's and the Plant Scientist's sets are dated logs running three weeks from May 6 to May 23.
    • Step 2: Brainstorm Ideas (p. 94). Sticky notes, aimed at how weather and environment will affect the drone.
    • Step 3: Design Your Drone (p. 95). Chart paper, every part labeled with a brief description.
    • Step 4: Get Peer Feedback (p. 95). A five-criterion form rated Yes, No, or Somewhat, with a notes column for the reviewer's reasoning.

    The activity runs Days 2 through 4 and puts students in the role of an Agricultural Engineer or Drone Operator, the same emerging careers the societal trends chart analyzes on Day 4. Its closing Class Discussion (p. 95) asks what work values matter in the role and what drones can and cannot help farmers with, which is the honest limit-of-technology beat this week needs.

    Three other sections of this chapter belong to the Agriculture weeks on either side of this one: "Grow System Rescue" (pp. 88-90) and "Farm to Table" (pp. 91-92) anchor Week 2, and "Powerskill: Critical Thinking" (pp. 96-99) anchors Week 1. One Powerskill lesson from a different chapter, Powerskill: Adaptability (FYF p. 146), backs Day 5: an outdoor concert is delayed 30 minutes by a tech failure, and the event planner has to keep the crowd engaged and write a stage announcement. It pairs directly with eDynamic Unit 7.1: Progressive and Adaptable.

    IISD Instructional Strategies

    • Think-Pair-Share: Day 4, Activity 2 and Activity 3, the societal trends chart rows and the class share that follows. Distinguished: after modeling the first two rows, pose the row-building question to the whole group, hold a silent 30 second Think, then time Partner A for 30 seconds and Partner B for 30 seconds before anyone writes, and post two stems at different levels: "Society is changing in the area of _, so a new career like is growing because __" and "One career that is not disappearing but changing is _, because workers now have to ___." Ask two pairs to share in Activity 3, not one. While pairs talk, listen for who can name the changing career and not just the new one, and pick your share-out pairs from what you heard rather than from raised hands.
    • Stop and Jot: Day 1, Activity 2, the two sentences students write under their note table after exploring NASA Climate Kids. Distinguished: name the stop point before students open the site, give 45 seconds per sentence with the stems already written into the plan on screen ("I read about _ on Climate Kids" and "This connects to the career of because they work on __"), and show one strong example before the timer starts. Read jots over shoulders and track which environmental problems students name. If nearly every jot says pollution, steer Activity 3's share toward water, heat, and food supply so Day 2's farm scenario is not the first time the class connects climate to growing food.
    • Active Monitoring: Day 3, Activity 2, the 25 minutes of chart paper drone design with the checkpoint clipboard. Distinguished: walk a fixed pathway three times, one lap per target, carrying the three-question check this week already uses (What sensors? How does it fly? How does it handle Texas weather?). Lap 1 at minute 8 looks for a drone shape with labeling started, lap 2 at minute 16 looks for six labels named, lap 3 at minute 22 looks for each label naming the field notes problem it solves. Have feedback ready before class for the two known misconceptions: a label that names a part but never says what it does, and a creative feature with no field note behind it. If more than a handful of designs have six labels and no problem links, stop the room and reproject one strong label instead of fixing it desk by desk.
    • Chunking: Day 2, Activity 2 and Activity 3, the three sets of field notes, plus the board work that opens Day 3. Distinguished: name the three chunks before any reading starts (Ag Engineer, then Farmer, then Plant Scientist), read one set at a time, and close each chunk with the one minute discussion and that source's row on the summary worksheet before the next set opens. Activity 3 then turns the three finished rows into the constraints list, which is the check that the chunks added up. Before the Day 3 design begins, walk the Engineering Design Process on the board (Define, Research, Plan and Design, Build and Prototype, Test, Improve) and mark where the class already stands. Note which set produced the thinnest rows. If the Ag Engineer rows are thin, re-teach the Drone Design Needs list at the top of Day 3 and hold the sticky note brainstorm open an extra minute for those students while the rest start designing.
    • Time, Voice, Body (TVB): Day 4, Activity 1, the four block peer feedback exchange. Distinguished: post the blocks before the first partner speaks, Voice 1 for the explaining turns and Voice 0 while partners fill the workbook form so reviewers are writing their own reasoning, and run a visible countdown for each block (6 minutes for Partner A, 6 for Partner B, 5 to complete the forms, 3 to write revisions in pen on the sketch). State where the drone designs sit while partners write and where completed forms go so the handoff does not eat the revision block. Narrate the pairs who are meeting the expectation rather than correcting the ones who are not. If pairs are running long on the first explaining turn, cut the second turn's warm-up talk and announce the change before Partner B starts.

    Formative Assessment

    • Environmental Hat notes + Mini-Case exit ticket (DFW lead-in-water; pick career + back with data + Climate Kids topic). Day 1, d(1)(C)
    • Field notes summary (one row per source) + constraints list + Ranked Justification exit ticket (4 drone constraints ranked). Day 2, d(1)(C)
    • Drone design + rationale + SCR exit ticket (feature + cut-feature + engineer task faster). Day 3, d(1)(C)
    • Workbook peer feedback form (FYF p. 95) + societal trends chart + Comparison Matrix exit ticket (2 societal changes x new/changing career). Day 4, d(1)(D), d(5)(C)
    • Xello Interests + eDynamic 7.1 + Concept Map exit ticket (3SW favorite career + Xello interest + Nimitz pathway + self-pattern). Day 5, d(1)(A), d(1)(C)

    Summative Assessment

    Ag-Tech Pest Patrol Drone Brief: Students submit (1) the labeled drone design with all required features (FYF p. 95, Step 3), (2) a 1-paragraph design rationale explaining how their drone solves the field notes problems, (3) the completed workbook peer feedback form with reviewer notes (FYF p. 95, Step 4), and (4) a societal trends chart with at least 5 rows showing how changing needs create new ag careers. Scored on engineering reasoning (does the design address pest detection?), connection to the Texas weather and farm size constraints in the Ag Engineer's notes (d(1)(D)), and societal-needs analysis depth (d(5)(C)).

    Differentiation

    Scaffolded Learning

    • Pre-printed drone outline with arrow lines showing where to label parts
    • Field notes summary worksheet with one row pre-filled as a model
    • Sentence stems for the design rationale paragraph
    • Pair work option for the drone design (designer + scribe)

    Extensions

    • Add a "drone version 2" design that handles a different Texas pest (e.g., grasshoppers in cotton fields, fire ants in pastures)
    • Calculate the cost of a real ag drone (DJI Agras T40 = ~$30,000) vs. the savings from earlier pest detection
    • Research one real ag-tech company in Texas (e.g., Hylio, Skydio) and read about their drones
    • Run "Powerskill: Adaptability" (FYF p. 146) for students who finish Day 5 early: brainstorm ways to keep a concert crowd engaged through a 30 minute tech delay, then write the stage announcement

    ELL Language Support

    • Pre-teach: Drone = Dron, Pest = Plaga, Sensor = Sensor, Engineer = Ingeniero/a, Crop = Cultivo
    • Bilingual field notes summary worksheet
    • The drone sketch is highly visual, students who struggle with English text can label parts with bilingual or Spanish-only annotations
    • Pair with bilingual peers during peer feedback
