    Day 4: NCCER, MacArthur Pathways, and Spot the Problem

    Lesson Overview
    Time 50 minutes
    Objectives Explore NCCER certifications and the MacArthur Construction Technology pathway; map a personal route from middle school to a construction career; record inspection observations on five images as a home inspector
    TEKS d(2)(A), d(3)(G), d(1)(C)
    5E Phases Engage: Warm-Up · Explain: NCCER credentials · Extend: MacArthur pathway map · Explore: Spot the Problem inspection · Evaluate: Exit Ticket
    Deliverable MacArthur pathway map + observations recorded for all five inspection images (FYF p. 177)
    Materials Chromebooks, Find Your Future workbook (printed pp. 176-178 and 196-197), Climber Notes deck "Spot the Problem" (slides 2-6), NCCER website, printed MacArthur pathway map, projector

    Warm-Up (5 min)

    WARM-UP: One new thing you learned about construction this week. Write it down in one sentence.

    Quick share from 3 volunteers. Most will name a wage, a trade, or the apprenticeship model. Bridge into the day: this morning is about the credential that gets a 17-year-old onto a job site, and this afternoon is about the person who inspects the work after the crew leaves.

    Activity 1: NCCER Overview (12 min)

    Source: nccer.org + (FYF pp. 196-197: "What is Happening at My District?")

    Introduce NCCER, the National Center for Construction Education and Research. NCCER creates the standardized curriculum used by most construction education programs across the country, including Irving ISD.

    Project the NCCER website and walk through the main credential structure:

    • NCCER CORE: The foundation credential. Covers basic safety, communication, employability, and hand tools. This is the entry-level NCCER cert that every construction worker needs.
    • NCCER Level 1, 2, 3, 4: Trade-specific credentials. Students take a Level 1 for their chosen trade (e.g., Carpentry Level 1), then progress through the levels as they gain experience.
    • NCCER Instructor Certification: Advanced credential for training others.

    Key point: MacArthur High School's Construction Technology pathway teaches NCCER CORE on campus, which means Irving ISD students can earn the foundation credential before they graduate from high school. A student who completes NCCER CORE in high school walks onto any construction job site with a nationally-recognized credential already in hand.

    The workbook's district pages list the same credentials from the district's side (FYF pp. 196-197): NCCER Core, Autodesk Revit, and the Structural Steel & Metal Welding Certification, with SkillsUSA as the student organization and a new Irving ISD CTE Center being built with welding and construction labs. The page also shows what the work looks like: one class designed and built a fully functioning tiny home from scratch, including the structural framing, HVAC, plumbing, and electrical installation.

    Activity 2: MacArthur Pathway Mapping (13 min)

    Distribute the MacArthur pathway map handout. Students trace the Irving ISD pathway from middle school to career:

    • Middle School (now): CCE course, explore A&C careers, complete the career research worksheet
    • High School at MacArthur: Construction Technology pathway → NCCER CORE certification
    • Post-high school options:
      • Direct to apprenticeship (union or non-union)
      • Community college construction program (Dallas College, Tarrant County College)
      • 4-year Construction Management degree (Texas A&M, UT Arlington)
      • Immediate employment as a helper or laborer with credential

    Students identify which post-high school path appeals to them most and why. They also identify the courses they would need to take in middle school to prepare: math (especially geometry), shop/CTE electives, English for communication skills.

    DOK 2: What is the advantage of starting NCCER CORE in HIGH SCHOOL versus waiting until after graduation? Name two specific benefits.

    Activity 3: Spot the Problem — Home Inspection (17 min)

    Source: (FYF pp. 176-178: "Spot the Problem", Steps 1-3), a Career Climb activity, and (Climber Notes: "Spot the Problem", slides 2-6)

    Step 1: Get to know the role (3 min). Open the workbook to page 176. A buyer is interested in a house at 1212 W Blue Rd and wants a full inspection before they decide. Home inspectors do not repair anything. They look closely, think carefully, and recommend which professionals to call in, which puts the job on the management and inspection side of construction rather than the tool side. Read the Step 1 watch-for list together so students know what counts as a clue:

    Area What inspectors watch for
    Structure Cracks in walls or foundations, uneven floors or walls
    Roof Missing or damaged shingles, sagging spots
    Water damage Stains on ceilings or walls, signs of mold or moisture
    Electrical Overloaded outlets, exposed wiring
    Insulation Areas where heat or cooled air may be escaping

    Step 2: Inspect the home (9 min). The five inspection images live in the teacher's Climber Notes deck, not in the student book, so project them one at a time. Hold each image for about 90 seconds while students record observations for that image in the boxes on page 177. Some images show real problems and some show a house in normal condition, so "this one looks normal" is a legitimate observation as long as the student says what they examined.

    Stop and Jot: one image, one jot, 90 seconds each, with the stem posted the whole time: "In Image #__ I notice _, which could mean ___." Read jots over shoulders while the image is still up. If most jots describe how the house looks rather than what a clue points to, pause after Image #2 and re-model one observation against the watch-for list before moving on.

    Step 3: Look beyond what you can see (5 min). Some problems hide behind walls and ceilings, which is why inspectors carry thermal (infrared) cameras. The camera shows temperature differences instead of light, so missing insulation, a hidden leak, or escaping air shows up as a patch of a different color. Students compare the regular and thermal images of the same wall and answer the three workbook questions on page 178: what looks different, what that difference might mean, and why this tool is useful.

    DOK 3: Some of today's images show a real problem and some show a house in normal condition. How did you decide which was which? Name the specific clue that moved one image from "normal" to "problem."

    Facilitation Tip

    Students who cannot name the defect still have the observation. Accept "the ceiling has a brown ring on it" and push for the second half with one question: "So what could be above that ceiling?" The report on Day 5 needs both halves, what they saw and why it matters, and the second half is the harder one.

    Exit Ticket (3 min)

    EXIT TICKET (Ranked Justification) · Printable PDF:

    The inspection at 1212 W Blue Rd found three problems. Rank them by which one the buyer should fix FIRST (1) to LAST (3).

    • Rank 1: _____ (exposed wiring in the garage / a crack in the foundation wall / missing shingles on the roof)

    • Rank 2: _____

    • Rank 3: _____

    For EACH rank, give the reason in one short sentence. Use safety, cost, or damage that gets worse over time:

    • Rank 1: _____________

    • Rank 2: _____________

    • Rank 3: _____________

    Bottom line: Which construction worker would I call for my Rank 1 problem? (electrician / roofer / mason / carpenter): _____

    ONE certification I could earn in HIGH SCHOOL in the MacArthur Construction Technology pathway: _____. (d(1)(C), d(2)(A))

    Differentiation

    • Support: Give the observation stem on a card for every image: "In Image #__ I see _. This could mean ___." Keep the five watch-for areas projected the whole time so students have the vocabulary in front of them, and let them tag an image by area (roof, water, electrical) before writing the sentence.
    • Extension: Students pick one image they flagged and research what a repair would cost, using contractor sites or CareerOneStop. Would this repair change what the buyer should pay for the house?
    • ELL: Pre-teach: Crack = Grieta, Leak = Fuga, Mold = Moho, Wiring = Cableado, Insulation = Aislamiento, Roof = Techo. The images carry the evidence visually, so students can record what they see before they have the English word for it. Allow observations in Spanish and translate them together into the report language on Day 5.
