    Week 6: Science Meets Medicine — Biomedical Careers

    2nd Six Weeks | Health Science Cluster | 5 class periods (50 min each)

    Lesson Objective

    Students explore the Biomedical Sciences pathway through Hats & Ladders and the Find Your Future workbook, design and label a medical nanobot in "Mini Medics," investigate a waterborne outbreak and build a public health response plan in "Outbreak Investigators," write a cover letter against a real job posting, research one emerging biomedical occupation, and finalize their Health Science cluster favorites in the Climber Profile.

    Learning Target: I can name at least three biomedical careers and the training each one needs, design a nanobot that meets the medical team's four requirements, find the source of a waterborne outbreak and plan how to stop it, and write a cover letter with all five sections.

    Success Criteria: I will know I am successful when my career research worksheet lists the job, the training, and the pay for one biomedical career, when my nanobot drawing is labeled with a size comparison and three clot-breaking tools, when my Investigation Report names at least three clues and one likely source, when my response plan has two immediate actions and one prevention step, and when my cover letter has all five sections.

    Demonstration of Learning

    "I can describe at least three biomedical careers, design and label a medical nanobot that meets a mission spec, investigate a waterborne outbreak and write a response plan for the community, write a professional cover letter, research an emerging biomedical occupation, and finalize my Health Science cluster favorites."

    TEKS Alignment

    • d(1)(C): Identify various career opportunities within the Health Science cluster (Days 1-5).
    • d(1)(D): Research and evaluate emerging occupations related to career interest areas (Day 5 Emerging Biomedical Career Research).
    • d(2)(A): Research and describe applicable academic, technical, certification, and training requirements (Day 5 template).
    • d(5)(A): Analyze labor market trends related to a career of interest (Day 5 BLS labor market row).
    • d(7)(B): Write business correspondence (Day 1 cover letter draft).

    Materials Needed

    • Chromebooks with internet access (1 per student)
    • Hats & Ladders student accounts + Find Your Future workbook, Health Science chapter (printed pp. 74-86)
    • Chart paper or poster board, markers, and rulers for Day 2 (FYF pp. 79-81: "Mini Medics")
    • Projector for the Fairview Edge image clues (FYF p. 75) and for reading workbook pages as a class
    • One real entry-level lab or clinic job posting for Day 1, pulled from a job site or a hospital careers page (FYF pp. 290-291: "Rung 5: Local Connections")
    • Printed cover letter worksheet (5 sections; CCE artifact)
    • Printed career research worksheet (CCE artifact; the research format is taught in Wk0)
    • Printed Emerging Biomedical Career Research template
    • BLS, Biomedical Engineers: bls.gov/ooh/architecture-and-engineering/biomedical-engineers.htm
    • BLS, Pharmacists: bls.gov/ooh/healthcare/pharmacists.htm
    • NIH STEM Teaching Resources: science.education.nih.gov
    • Xello accounts (Save Careers activity)

    Career Connection

    Biomedical engineers design artificial organs, prosthetics, and devices such as the nanobots students prototype on Day 2. Lab technicians help develop drugs and vaccines. Epidemiologists trace outbreaks to their source so public health teams can stop further illness. Many of these careers require a master's or doctoral degree. By the end of this cluster wrap-up, students identify which Health Science pathways fit their interests and update the Career Plan they have built across two six-week periods.

    What is Happening at Irving ISD? Biomedical Sciences at Irving High School is the district program of study behind this week. The workbook's district pages describe it as preparing students for health care careers by studying human body systems, disease, medical research and treatment, and laboratory procedures while building scientific investigation and critical thinking skills (FYF pp. 84-85: "What is Happening at My District?"). Those pages also name the industry certifications Health Science students can earn, including the Biotechnology Aptitude and Competency Exam, Patient Care Technician, Certified Registered Dental Assistant, Phlebotomy, and Billing and Coding Specialist, and they name HOSA as the student organization. Health Science students at Singley Academy job-shadow at local hospitals and dental offices and practice on programmable patient simulators that display changing vital signs. The same pages run a Career Exploration Connection on financial resources worth five minutes of Day 1: grants and scholarships usually do not have to be repaid, while loans are borrowed money that does.

    Vocabulary

    • Biomedical Engineering: The application of engineering principles to medicine and biology, designing medical devices, prosthetics, and drug delivery systems.
    • Biotechnology: The use of living organisms and biological systems to develop products and technologies for healthcare, agriculture, and industry.
    • Nanobot: A machine small enough to travel inside the human body, designed to carry medicine, break up blockages, or repair damaged tissue.
    • Epidemiologist: A scientist who studies how and why people get sick and how illness spreads through a community.
    • Waterborne Illness: Sickness caused by germs in drinking water, often after flooding mixes dirty water into a clean water source.
    • Clinical Trial: A research study that tests new medical treatments or drugs on human volunteers to determine safety and effectiveness.
    • Research Scientist: A professional who conducts experiments to advance knowledge in a specific field like medicine, biology, or chemistry.
    • Emerging Occupation: A new or rapidly growing career that reflects current trends in technology, science, or society.
    • Cover Letter: A short personalized letter sent with a resume that introduces a job applicant to an employer.

    Bridge to Theory (Hats & Ladders)

    The Find Your Future workbook gives Health Science printed pages 59-86, shared across the four weeks of this cluster. Weeks 3 and 4 work the front of the chapter. This week takes the back half, where the activities move from treating one patient to designing devices and protecting whole communities.

    This week uses two chapter activities:

    • Mini Medics (Day 2): Career Climb activity. Students are nanobot engineers designing a robot small enough to travel through the bloodstream and break up a dangerous blood clot without damaging the vessel wall. They plan the robot, size it against something familiar, name three clot-breaking tools, draw and label it on chart paper, map its journey through the body, and pick the strongest design in their group (FYF pp. 79-81: "Mini Medics").
    • Outbreak Investigators (Days 3-4): Career Climb activity. Students are environmental epidemiologists investigating a waterborne outbreak in Fairview Edge, a town of 2,000 that recently flooded. They examine a case file of eight residents against a set of environmental clues, complete an Investigation Report, analyze the pattern and severity of the illness, and build a response plan covering confirming tests, estimated impact, immediate actions, and prevention (FYF pp. 74-78: "Outbreak Investigators").

    Day 1's app work follows the cluster's own App Exploration page (FYF p. 86: "App Exploration"), which sends students to Pathway Possibilities, one rated pathway, and three rated Hats. Because this is the cluster's fourth week, students skip the cluster tour they already took in Week 3 and go straight to the pathway and rating steps, then finish the ratings on Day 5. The cover letter has no workbook activity behind it; it is a CCE artifact, and the "what employers are looking for" input comes from the Capstone rung students will complete for themselves in 6th Six Weeks (FYF pp. 290-291: "Rung 5: Local Connections").

    A third chapter activity, Patient Education (FYF pp. 82-83), puts partners on a hospital patient-education team writing AI prompts for a labeled medical illustration, then judging what the AI got right and wrong. It runs as the Day 5 extension and needs a campus-approved AI image tool.

    IISD Instructional Strategies

    • Think-Pair-Share: Day 3, Activity 2, the Fairview Edge case file, which the workbook itself pairs students for. Distinguished: hold a silent 30 second Think while students read the eight-resident table alone, then time Partner A for 30 seconds on the pattern they see and Partner B for 30 seconds, and post two stems at different levels: "Everyone who got sick _" and "The one thing the people who did NOT get sick have in common is ___." Two pairs share before anyone writes. Listen during pair talk for who names Ethan and Lilly, the two bottled-water residents who stayed healthy, and choose your share-out pairs from what you heard rather than from raised hands. If no pair reaches the healthy residents, point the whole room at those two rows before releasing the Investigation Report.
    • Stop and Jot: Day 1, Activity 2, the NIH scientist video. Distinguished: name both stop points before the clip starts, give 45 seconds per jot with the prompt on screen, and offer two stems: "One technology this scientist uses is _" and "One quality this scientist shows is , and I saw it when __." Read jots over shoulders during both pauses and track which qualities students name. If nearly every jot says "smart," push the cover letter Body Paragraph work in Activity 3 toward the qualities employers actually list in the posting, such as attention to detail and following a procedure exactly.
    • Active Monitoring: Day 4, Activities 1 and 2, the four parts of the Fairview Edge response plan. Distinguished: walk one lap per part with a single target each. Lap 1 target: does the pair's investigation-steps list include at least one water test AND the sick-to-healthy comparison? Lap 2 target: does the estimated-impact reasoning cite the 2,000-resident population or the shared water system? Lap 3 target: does each checked immediate action carry a written reason? Lap 4 target: does the prevention plan name one step the pair would fund first? Have feedback ready before class for the two predictable misses: pairs who check every box and explain none, and pairs who estimate "thousands" in a town of 2,000. If more than a handful miss the population check, pause and reproject the Fairview Edge background paragraph instead of correcting pair by pair.
    • Chunking: Day 2, Activity 2, the three Mini Medics steps. Distinguished: run plan, then draw and label, then journey, and state what each chunk is for before releasing it (the plan sets the specs, the drawing proves the specs, the journey proves the mission). Close each chunk with the check already written into the plan: every team has a size comparison and three named tools before drawing starts, and every label carries a purpose before the journey starts. Note which check holds teams up. If most teams stall on the size comparison, run a 2 minute whole-class round of comparisons before the drawing chunk, and release teams that already cleared it to start labeling early.
    • Time, Voice, Body (TVB): Day 2, Activity 2, the chart-paper nanobot drawing. Distinguished: post the three blocks before any marker comes off the table. Voice 1 for team drawing talk and Voice 0 for the last two minutes of labeling, markers capped between uses, chart paper flat on the desk rather than on the floor, and a visible 14 minute countdown on the screen. Narrate the teams meeting the expectation rather than correcting the ones who are not. If the room is losing drawing time to marker hunting, move a marker set to each table before the countdown starts and say why you moved it.

    Summative Assessment

    2nd Six Weeks Portfolio Update (Day 5): Students submit (1) the completed Emerging Biomedical Career Research template, (2) their updated My Career Journey reflection covering both 1SW and 2SW, and (3) their refined SMART goal from Week 5. The portfolio shows their growth across two complete six-week periods. Scored on: emerging career research quality (d(1)(D)), labor market analysis (d(5)(A)), education pathway knowledge (d(2)(A)), and self-reflection growth.

    Differentiation

    Scaffolded Learning

    • Cover letter sentence stems for each of the 5 sections
    • Pre-drawn nanobot outline students label rather than drawing from scratch, plus a size-comparison word bank and a clot-tool word bank
    • Fairview Edge case-file table with the water-source and symptoms-started columns pre-highlighted so students compare the two variables that matter
    • Response plan with the Helpful Info for Investigators facts reprinted at the top of the page
    • Pre-filled emerging career research template with one career modeled

    Extensions

    • "Patient Education" (FYF pp. 82-83): partners write, test, and revise AI prompts for a labeled medical illustration, then judge accuracy against what a patient would actually need
    • Research a specific DFW biotech employer (McKesson, Kimberly-Clark Health, UT Southwestern Medical Center) and identify what careers they hire for
    • Calculate the total education cost for a Biomedical Engineer and find the salary break-even point
    • Build a 4-year plan for the Irving High Biomedical Sciences pathway including AP Biology and dual credit options
    • Write the one-paragraph public notice Fairview Edge would post at the grocery store and the school

    ELL Language Support

    • Pre-teach: Biomedical = Biomédico, Research = Investigación, Laboratory = Laboratorio, Emerging = Emergente, Cover Letter = Carta de Presentación, Outbreak = Brote, Blood clot = Coágulo
    • Bilingual labels allowed on the nanobot drawing; the drawing carries most of the meaning, so students can present visually and name parts in either language
    • Bilingual Fairview Edge case-file table with the symptom words translated
    • Pair ELL students with bilingual peers for the Day 3 Think-Pair-Share and the Day 4 presentations
    • The workbook reads well above grade level, so read the scenario paragraphs aloud and encourage browser translation for any independent reading
