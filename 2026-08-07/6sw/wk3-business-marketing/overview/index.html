    Week 3: Sell It — Business Marketing Careers

    6th Six Weeks | Marketing Cluster | 5 class periods (50 min each)

    Lesson Objective

    Students explore Marketing careers within the Business, Marketing, and Finance cluster through the Hats & Ladders app and the Find Your Future workbook, write call-to-action ad copy for four products and build an ad mock-up in "Click Factor," draft a Little Library social media post in "Powerskill: Written Communication," analyze how economic conditions affect marketing employment, turn one personal skill into a consulting business in "Expert Edge," and read three marketing data sets to choose and defend a campaign strategy in "Powerskill: Data-Informed Decision Making."

    Learning Target: I can name at least three marketing careers, write a call to action that fits a product and its audience, build a consulting service I could really offer, and use data to pick a marketing strategy.

    Success Criteria: I will know I am successful when my ad mock-up has a headline, a short description, and my chosen call to action, when my consulting page names the service, the offer, the price, and carries a logo, when my Family Fun Pass pitch names the strategy I picked and the numbers behind it, and when my economic conditions chart is filled in for both a strong and a weak economy.

    Demonstration of Learning

    "I can describe at least three marketing careers, explain how economic changes affect marketing job availability, write a call to action that matches a product and its audience, and design a consulting service with a name, an offer, a price, and a logo."

    TEKS Alignment

    • d(1)(C): Identify various career opportunities within the Marketing cluster (Days 1-5; researched on Day 4).
    • d(3)(I): Define entrepreneurship and identify entrepreneurial opportunities in marketing (Day 3 consulting business, Day 5 pitch).
    • d(5)(C): Analyze effects of changing societal needs and economic conditions on employment trends and career choices (Day 2 economic conditions chart).

    Materials Needed

    • Chromebooks with internet access (1 per student)
    • Hats & Ladders student accounts + Find Your Future workbook, Business, Marketing, and Finance chapter (printed pp. 221-254)
    • Find Your Future workbook pp. 147-148, "Powerskill: Written Communication" (Powerskills in Action chapter, not the Business chapter)
    • BLS, Market Research Analysts: bls.gov/ooh/business-and-financial/market-research-analysts.htm
    • Google Applied Digital Skills: applieddigitalskills.withgoogle.com (Day 4 extension)
    • eDynamic Learning Unit 4.1: Enterprise and Money Matters
    • Xello School Subjects at Work lesson (supplemental; not a Bowie Grade 8 completion standard)
    • Printed blank ad mock-up sheets (1 per student) for "Click Factor" Step 4 (FYF p. 230)
    • Sticky notes for the "Expert Edge" skill brainstorm (FYF p. 222) and the Little Library brainstorm (FYF p. 147)
    • Printed marketing career research worksheet (CCE artifact; the research format is taught in Wk0)
    • Printed Economic Conditions Analysis chart (CCE artifact)
    • Canva for Education (ad mock-ups, logos, social posts)

    Career Connection

    Marketing is the work of finding an audience and giving that audience a reason to act. In DFW, that work happens inside corporations such as AT&T, American Airlines, Toyota, Mary Kay, and Frito-Lay, at marketing and advertising agencies, and through freelance work. Students compare Marketing Manager and Brand Manager with digital roles such as Social Media Manager, Content Creator, and SEO Specialist.

    What is Happening at Irving ISD? Business, Marketing and Finance (Business Management plus Marketing and Sales) is offered at Irving High School and Nimitz High School, with Entrepreneurship & Small Business as its industry certification. MacArthur High School runs its own School of Business and Entrepreneurship, holding Administrative Management, Entrepreneurship, Real Estate Marketing, and Sales Management. Cardwell Career Preparatory Center offers Business Management and Entrepreneurship through its School of Workforce Readiness. Marketing is not offered at Singley Academy. The workbook's district pages describe a Marketing & Sales program of study built on market research, advertising, branding, product promotion, and data-driven decision making; students who have produced content, photography, and social media pitches for Irving ISD events; student-operated school stores; and two student organizations, DECA and Business Professionals of America (BPA) (FYF pp. 252-253: "What is Happening at My District?"). Those pages also name a Retail Management program of study that no campus page lists, so treat that one as [VERIFY with CTE coordinator] before telling students where to take it.

    Vocabulary

    • Marketing: The process of promoting, selling, and distributing a product or service. Includes advertising, market research, branding, and social media.
    • Market Research: The process of gathering information about consumers' needs and preferences to guide business decisions.
    • Target Audience: The specific group of people a marketing campaign is designed to reach.
    • Call to Action (CTA): The part of an ad that tells the audience what to do next, like "Buy Now" or "Sign up today." The five types students use this week are Benefit, Urgency, Curiosity, Direct Command, and Incentive.
    • Mock-Up: A rough draft or sketch of an ad that shows the headline, the description, and where the call to action sits before the real ad is built.
    • Consultant: Someone who sells skill and knowledge instead of a product. Consultants listen to a client, figure out what the client needs, and deliver advice, coaching, or a plan.
    • Mission Statement: One or two sentences naming the purpose of a business and who it serves.
    • Data-Informed Decision Making: Using real information, not guesses, to choose what to do. Marketers use it to pick which campaign to run.
    • Economic Conditions: The state of the economy at a given time, including factors like employment rates, consumer spending, and business growth.

    Bridge to Theory (Hats & Ladders)

    The Find Your Future workbook gives Business, Marketing, and Finance printed pages 221 to 254. The chapter opener (FYF p. 221: "Exploring the World of BUSINESS, MARKETING, AND FINANCE") frames the cluster as helping organizations grow, connect with customers, and make smart financial decisions, puts a "Be the Decision Maker" spending dilemma in front of students, and introduces three Hats on the page: Business Analyst, Financial Examiner, and E-Commerce Specialist. The chapter prints no pathway list. Students meet the cluster's pathways in the Hats & Ladders app through the Pathway Possibilities step on the App Exploration page (FYF p. 254: "App Exploration"), and they meet Irving ISD's own programs of study on the district pages (FYF pp. 252-253: "What is Happening at My District?").

    Four workbook activities carry this week:

    • Click Factor (Day 1): Career Climb activity. Students are digital marketing specialists whose team needs ads that make people act, not just look. They find and compare the call to action in two printed ads, then write two contrasting CTAs for each of four products (Local Car Wash, Cooking Class, After-School Snack Box, Fishing Gear) using a five-type reference table, pair up with another group to pick the strongest CTA per product, and build an ad mock-up with a headline, a short description, and their chosen CTA (FYF pp. 225-227 and 230: "Click Factor").
    • Powerskill: Written Communication (Day 2): Students run the social media account for a neighborhood Little Library. They brainstorm the library's status on sticky notes, then write one post that states the status clearly, carries a call to action, and uses at least two hashtags, against the workbook's four tips for effective written communication (FYF pp. 147-148: "Powerskill: Written Communication"). This lesson sits in the Powerskills in Action chapter, so the page jump is backward from the Business chapter.
    • Expert Edge (Day 3): Career Climb activity. Students open a consulting business built on a skill they already have. They brainstorm three skills on sticky notes and name who needs each one, choose one to build on, then write the service name, the offer, a mission statement, a price, and one extra add-on, design a logo, and pitch the service to a partner who rates it for clarity, creativity, and persuasiveness (FYF pp. 222-224: "Expert Edge").
    • Powerskill: Data-Informed Decision Making (Day 4): Teams launching a "Family Fun Pass" family subscription read three data sets, an audience preference table by age, a past campaign performance table, and five focus group quotes, choose one marketing strategy from six, and build a short pitch naming the strategy, the data behind it, and why families would respond (FYF pp. 229 and 228: "Powerskill: Data-Informed Decision Making").

    Two workbook pages are printed out of order

    Pages 228 and 230 were transposed at the printer. "Click Factor" runs 225, 226, 227, and then 230 for Step 4. "Powerskill: Data-Informed Decision Making" opens on 229 and continues on 228 for Steps 2, 3, and 4. Tell students the page jump before they open the book so nobody loses activity time hunting for the next step.

    The rest of this chapter is taught in other weeks and referenced here, not adopted: Million Dollar Idea (FYF pp. 234-237) ran in 3SW Wk6, Flip This House (FYF pp. 238-240) and Powerskill: Visual Storytelling (FYF pp. 231-233) ran in 5SW Wk6, and the 30-second sales pitch (FYF pp. 241-243), Powerskill: Problem Solving (FYF pp. 244-247), and Make the Call: CEO Edition (FYF pp. 248-251) sit in the Sales and Presentations week that follows this one. Day 5's Mini Marketing Plan pitch is a CCE-authored structure, not a workbook activity, and every line of it traces back to the student's own Click Factor, Expert Edge, and Family Fun Pass pages.

    IISD Instructional Strategies

    • Think-Pair-Share: Day 1, Activity 2, Step 1 of "Click Factor" (FYF p. 225), where partners hunt the call to action in the two printed ads. Distinguished: release the workbook's four questions one at a time, hold a silent 30 second Think on which CTA feels stronger, then time Partner A for 30 seconds and Partner B for 30 seconds, and post two stems at different levels: "Ad ___ has the stronger call to action because _" and "Ad ___ fits its product better because its audience is and it asks them to __." Ask two pairs to share, not one. Listen while pairs talk for who argues from the audience rather than from the artwork, and pick your share-out pairs from what you heard rather than from raised hands.
    • Stop and Jot: Day 1, Activity 1, the two pauses in the Business, Marketing, and Finance cluster tour video and the workbook's own "What is one thing that you learned?" line on the App Exploration page (FYF p. 254). Distinguished: name both stop points before the video starts, give 45 seconds per jot with the prompt on screen, and offer two stems: "One marketing career I did not know existed is _" and "One question I still have about how companies choose their ads is ___." Read jots over shoulders during both pauses and track which careers get named. If most jots say Social Media Manager only, steer the Hat Finder browse toward Market Research Analyst, Business Analyst, and E-Commerce Specialist so Day 4's research worksheets are not five copies of the same career.
    • Active Monitoring: Day 3, Activity 1, the four steps of "Expert Edge" (FYF pp. 222-224). Distinguished: walk a fixed pathway four times, one lap per step, each lap carrying a single target (lap 1 three skills written with a named person who needs each one, lap 2 an offer that names something the client receives, lap 3 a logo readable at arm's length, lap 4 a pitch that lands under two minutes). Carry the workbook's own partner feedback categories on the clipboard, Clarity, Creativity, and Persuasiveness, and mark them as you pass. Have the feedback ready before class for the two predictable misses: an offer written as a topic ("I help with math") instead of a service, and a price with no unit attached. If more than a handful of students miss the lap 2 target, pause the room and rewrite one volunteer's offer on the projector instead of fixing it desk by desk.
    • Chunking: Day 4, Activity 2, the three data sets in Step 1 of "Powerskill: Data-Informed Decision Making" (FYF p. 229). Distinguished: release one data set at a time, say what each one answers before students read it (the age table says who prefers which channel, the campaign table says what actually sold, the focus group quotes say why), and take the thumbs check between sets before releasing the next. Note which set draws the most thumbs down. If it is the campaign table, spend the re-teach on the gap between clicks and sales before Step 2 opens, and let the groups who cleared all three sets start drafting their pitch early instead of waiting.
    • Time, Voice, Body (TVB): Day 5, Activity 1, the one-minute Mini Marketing Plan pitches. Distinguished: post the three blocks before the first pitch, Voice 1 for drafting and partner practice, Voice 0 while a group member is pitching, Voice 2 for the Two Stars and a Wish feedback, and run the visible 60 second countdown already written into the plan for every pitch. State where presenters stand and where notecards go so the rotation does not eat pitch time. Narrate the groups that are meeting the expectation rather than correcting the ones that are not. If the third pitch in a group is running long, cut feedback to one star and announce the change before the next presenter starts.

    Summative Assessment

    Marketing Plan Pitch (Day 5): Students present a 1-minute Mini Marketing Plan summary that includes: product or service name, target audience, marketing channel, key message, and economic justification ("This will sell because..."). Most students pitch the consulting service they built in "Expert Edge" on Day 3; the marketing channel comes from the same six-option list they worked in the Family Fun Pass activity on Day 4. Scored on marketing career knowledge (d(1)(C)), entrepreneurship application (d(3)(I)), and economic awareness (d(5)(C)).

    Differentiation

    Scaffolded Learning

    • Provide three sample CTAs per product for "Click Factor" Step 2, already labeled by type, so students revise instead of starting from a blank line
    • Provide an "Expert Edge" starter list of eight consulting skills (gaming coach, homework tutor, sneaker cleaning, phone photography, art commissions, party DJ, pet sitting, bike repair) so students choose rather than invent
    • Highlight the two largest numbers in each column of the Family Fun Pass data tables so students start from the pattern instead of hunting for it
    • Allow students to present their Mini Marketing Plan in written format if oral presentation is too challenging

    Extensions

    • Students write CTAs for a fifth product of their own choosing and label which of the five types each one uses
    • Research a real DFW marketing agency (Tracy Locke, The Richards Group) and present what services it offers
    • Students who finish "Expert Edge" early add a referral offer to their service and explain what it costs them and what it earns them
    • Design TWO ad mock-ups for the same product targeting two different audiences and explain what changed and why

    ELL Language Support

    • Pre-teach: Marketing = Mercadotecnia, Brand = Marca, Target Audience = Audiencia objetivo, Call to Action = Llamada a la acción, Consultant = Consultor, Data = Datos
    • Bilingual ad mock-up and social post templates with Spanish CTA options
    • Students may write the campaign in Spanish or make it bilingual; DFW audiences encounter marketing in both languages
    • The workbook reads well above grade level, so read the scenario paragraphs aloud and encourage browser translation for any independent reading
    • Pair ESL students with bilingual peers during pitch presentations
