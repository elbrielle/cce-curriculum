# Teacher-Facing Voice Standard

Use this standard for prose under `docs/`. Curriculum structure still follows `CLAUDE.md`, `editing-heuristics.md`, and the relevant authoring rulebook. This file governs the sentences inside that structure.

## The reader

Write for a teacher preparing to teach, often with limited time and no prior knowledge of the lesson. Give the teacher the fact, decision, example, or classroom move they need. Do not advertise the lesson to them.

The voice should be direct, observant, and specific. It may be formal because this is district curriculum, but it should still sound like one teacher explaining the work to another.

## What to write

- Begin with the actual classroom situation or subject matter. Name the activity, material, misconception, or decision.
- Prefer plain verbs: `is`, `has`, `uses`, `shows`, `writes`, `builds`, `checks`.
- Repeat the correct noun when repetition keeps the reference clear. Do not rename the same thing for variety.
- Let details carry the importance. If a fact matters, say what changes for the teacher or student because of it.
- Preserve useful friction. A sentence can be long when its parts depend on one another and the rhythm helps the reader follow the thought.
- Use a short sentence when it earns the stop.
- Keep uncertainty attached to the exact unknown. Use `[VERIFY]` markers for unresolved platform or district facts; do not guess around them.

## What to cut or rebuild

- Claims that an activity is `valuable`, `meaningful`, `authentic`, `robust`, or `real-world` without saying what students actually do or learn.
- Commentary tacked onto a fact with `ensuring`, `highlighting`, `emphasizing`, `fostering`, or similar language.
- Empty contrasts such as `not just X, but Y` when nobody believes X.
- Promotional lines that praise a week, career, platform, or pathway instead of describing it.
- Artificial sets of three. Use the number the lesson requires.
- Lead-ins that only announce the list or table immediately below them.
- Stiff substitutes for `is`, `are`, `has`, `uses`, or `shows`.
- Claims about what `real professionals` do unless the sentence names a sourced practice.

## Protected curriculum language

An editorial pass does not change TEKS codes, Learning Targets, Success Criteria, 5E sequences, timings, material quantities, citations, platform markers, student prompts, exit tickets, deliverables, or assessment criteria. If one of these appears wrong, log it as a curriculum issue and handle it through its governing audit process.

District terms such as `Think-Pair-Share`, `Stop and Jot`, `Active Monitoring`, `Chunking`, `Time, Voice, Body (TVB)`, and `Distinguished` remain exact. Their surrounding prose should still be plain and concrete.

## Revision test

For each paragraph, ask:

1. Could this paragraph appear nearly unchanged in a different week? If so, replace it with the week-specific fact or cut it.
2. Does the paragraph tell the teacher what happens, what to watch for, or what to do next? If not, it is probably commentary.
3. Did the revision preserve every instructional constraint and source claim?
4. Does it sound like a teacher who has thought through the room, or like copy selling the idea of teaching?

Specificity is the target. Concision is useful only when it does not remove the reason the sentence exists.
