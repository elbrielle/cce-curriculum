Model the quality target

Complete model

Career: Veterinary Technician
Detail: May work evening or weekend emergency shifts.
Mark: Quirk
Why: I prefer a predictable daytime schedule, so changing hours would be difficult for me.
Source: Hats & Ladders career profile.

Non-model

“Career: Vet. Detail: Good job. Mark: Perk. Why: I like it.”

Ask, “What information is missing?” Listen for: a specific career fact, a connection to a personal value or preference, and a usable source.

50-minute lesson flow

1. Welcome and Do Now · 5 minutes

  • Welcome students to class and ask them to open FYF to p. 4.
  • Project: “A job requires working outside during a Texas summer. Is that a perk, neutral, or quirk for you? Why?”
  • Give one quiet minute, then ask for thumbs up, flat hand, or thumbs down.
  • Invite two students with different answers to explain.
  • Say, “The work condition stayed the same. Our preferences changed the classification.”

2. Teach the three classifications · 10 minutes

  • Use the FYF p. 4 Pest Control Technician example.
  • Define perk as a detail that fits what a person values or prefers.
  • Define neutral as a detail that neither attracts nor discourages the person.
  • Define quirk as a demanding or unusual detail that may not fit the person’s preferences.
  • Point to a duty, schedule, and work-setting detail. Ask, “Could two people mark this differently? What value would explain each answer?”
  • Clarify that a quirk is a tradeoff to understand, not an automatic reason to reject a career.

3. Guided model and oral rehearsal · 10 minutes

  • Read the Veterinary Technician detail aloud.
  • Think aloud: “I notice the changing schedule. I value predictable hours. That evidence makes this a quirk for me.”
  • Contrast: “Someone who values variety might call the same schedule a perk.”
  • Show the pattern: specific fact → P/N/Q mark → personal value or preference → source.
  • Partners rehearse: “I marked ___ as a ___ because I value/prefer ___.”
  • Ask one partner to share while the other points to the supporting career fact.

4. Independent career analysis · 20 minutes

  • Students choose one career from an FYF profile, a prior H&L recommendation, or the teacher-provided profile.
  • Students record at least four specific details across duties, schedule, work setting, training, or tools.
  • Students mark every detail as a perk, neutral, or quirk for themselves.
  • Students explain every mark with a work value or preference and name the source.
  • At minute 30, scan for vague words such as “fun,” “easy,” or “good.” Ask, “What does the worker actually do, use, study, or experience?”
  • At minute 40, students star their strongest row and rehearse it with a partner.

5. Demonstration of Learning and cleanup · 5 minutes

  • Ask students to confirm: four specific details; a P/N/Q mark and personal reason for each; one named source.
  • Invite one student to finish: “A career detail is not automatically a perk or a quirk because…”
  • Students return workbooks to the class shelf with p. 5 open for a quick teacher scan.
Language, access, and recovery supports
  • Word bank: perk, neutral, quirk, duty, schedule, work setting, training, work value, preference.
  • Visual cue: thumbs up = perk, flat hand = neutral, thumbs down = quirk.
  • Optional stem: “I marked ___ as a ___ because I value/prefer ___.”
  • Home-language support: Pair home-language equivalents for the three classification words when useful; keep the grade-level thinking target.
  • Workbook unavailable: Use the equivalent five-column paper table and do not require later recopying.
  • Absent: Read the FYF p. 4 model and complete one p. 5 table. No Xello catch-up belongs to this lesson.
