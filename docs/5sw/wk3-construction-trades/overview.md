# Week 3: Built to Last — Construction Careers

**5th Six Weeks** | Architecture & Construction Cluster | 5 class periods (50 min each)

## Lesson Objective

Students explore construction careers through Hats & Ladders and the *Find Your Future* workbook, investigate trade school and apprenticeship pathways, research trade unions and professional associations, classify construction occupations as high-skill, high-wage, and high-demand using real DFW labor market data, and work a home inspection in "Spot the Problem," recording what they observe and writing a professional inspection report.

**Learning Target:** I can name three construction careers and the training each one needs, explain how an apprenticeship works, name one trade union and what it does for its members, use pay and job growth data to say whether a construction career is high-skill, high-wage, or high-demand, and write an inspection report on a house.

**Success Criteria:** I will know I am successful when my research sheet lists the job, the training, the pay, and the tools for one construction career, when my chart shows the cost, the length, the training wage, and the debt for both an apprenticeship and a 4-year college, when my classification worksheet gives a number as evidence for each of the five careers, and when my inspection report names what I saw in all five images and says why each one matters.

## Demonstration of Learning

> "I can describe at least three construction careers, explain the apprenticeship model, identify the role of trade unions, classify construction careers using high-skill / high-wage / high-demand criteria, and write a home inspection report that names each problem and why it matters."

## Career Connection

Construction employers report more openings than trained workers available to fill them. In DFW, population growth keeps carpenters, masons, heavy equipment operators, and construction managers working on new buildings and infrastructure. Students will check the actual pay and projected-growth numbers rather than treating "high demand" as a slogan.

The **apprenticeship model** is the traditional entry point for most construction careers. Apprentices learn on the job, are paid while they train, and progress through journeyman and master levels. Put that route beside a four-year degree: the time, cost, classroom hours, and point at which a student begins earning are different.

**What is Happening at Irving ISD?** **Construction Technology** at **MacArthur High School** (School of Architecture, Construction and Civil Engineering, or ACE) leads to the National Center for Construction Education & Research (NCCER) Core certification. **Welding**, also within the ACE school at MacArthur, leads to the Structural Steel & Metal Welding Certification.

The workbook's district pages add the local detail students respond to most (FYF pp. 196-197: "What is Happening at My District?"). The Career Exploration Connection for this cluster is **Work Readiness**, which covers introducing yourself, communicating professionally, and preparing for an opportunity. The three programs of study are Architecture, Construction Technology, and Welding. The industry-based certifications named are NCCER Core, Autodesk Revit, and the Structural Steel & Metal Welding Certification, and the student organization is **SkillsUSA**. The page also previews the new Irving ISD CTE Center, which is being built with welding and construction labs, and shows a class that built a fully functioning tiny home from scratch, doing their own design, structural framing, HVAC, plumbing, and electrical installation.

## Vocabulary

- **NCCER:** National Center for Construction Education and Research. NCCER develops standardized curriculum and certifications for all construction trades.
- **Apprenticeship:** Paid training that combines on-the-job experience with classroom instruction. Typically 2-5 years long.
- **Journeyman:** A fully trained worker who has completed an apprenticeship and can work independently.
- **Master:** The highest level of trade certification. Masters can start their own business and train other apprentices.
- **Trade Union:** An organization of workers that negotiates wages, benefits, and working conditions. Unions also run apprenticeship programs.
- **High-Demand Occupation:** A career with more job openings than qualified workers.
- **Home Inspector:** The professional a buyer hires to examine a house before they purchase it. Inspectors do not make repairs. They find problems and name the trades that should be called in.
- **Thermal (Infrared) Camera:** A camera that shows temperature differences instead of light. Inspectors use one to find missing insulation, hidden leaks, and air loss behind a wall.

## Bridge to Theory (Hats & Ladders)

The *Find Your Future* workbook gives Architecture & Construction printed pages 171-198, shared across Weeks 1 through 4 of this six weeks. The chapter does not print a pathway list. Students meet the construction pathways two ways instead: the Pathway Possibilities step in the Hats & Ladders app (FYF p. 198: "App Exploration") and the PROGRAMS OF STUDY sidebar on the district pages (FYF pp. 196-197), which names Irving ISD's own Architecture, Construction Technology, and Welding programs. Week 3 works the building trades and construction management; Week 4 works the skilled trades.

Two chapter sections carry this week:

- **Spot the Problem** (Days 4-5): Career Climb activity. Students take the role of a home inspector at 1212 W Blue Rd. They learn what inspectors watch for in structure, roof, water damage, electrical, and insulation, record observations on five inspection images, compare a regular and a thermal image of the same wall, and write a formal inspection report (FYF pp. 176-179: "Spot the Problem"). The five images live in the teacher's Climber Notes deck, not in the student book, so the teacher projects them.
- **Powerskill: Creativity** (Day 5 extension): students are the mason hired to design a mosaic mural for a downtown revitalization project, working to a written brief of at least three colors, one repeating pattern, one curve or diagonal, and a 12-foot wall laid out on a grid where each square is one 6-inch tile (FYF pp. 191-193: "Powerskill: Creativity"). Mason is one of this week's jigsaw trades.

Students met **Unexpected Architecture** (FYF pp. 182-184) in Week 1, so it is a cross-reference here rather than a repeat.

## IISD Instructional Strategies

- **Think-Pair-Share:** Day 2, Activity 3, where each student pairs with a classmate who researched a different trade union. Distinguished: pose the question to the whole group, hold a silent 30 second Think, then time Partner A for 30 seconds and Partner B for 30 seconds, and post two stems at different levels: "Both unions give members _____" and "I would apply to _____ instead of _____ because _____." Ask two pairs to share, not one. Listen while pairs talk for who names a benefit beyond wages, and pick your share-out pairs from what you heard rather than from raised hands. If most pairs compare only starting wage, open Day 3 by modeling training length as high-skill evidence so the classification worksheet does not collapse into a salary contest.
- **Stop and Jot:** Day 4, Activity 3, the five Climber Notes inspection images. Distinguished: name the routine before the first image goes up, hold each image for 90 seconds with the stem posted, and offer two stems: "In Image #__ I notice _____, which could mean _____" and "Image #__ looks normal to me because _____." Read jots over shoulders while each image is on screen. If most jots describe how the house looks rather than what a clue points to, pause after Image #2 and re-model one observation using the Step 1 watch-for list before continuing.
- **Active Monitoring:** Day 3, Activity 2, the classification worksheet. Distinguished: walk a fixed pathway three times, one lap per target (lap 1 at minute 10, every salary cell carries a source; lap 2 at minute 20, every career has a BLS growth percentage; lap 3, the written justification names at least one number), marking a clipboard as you go. Have the feedback ready before class for the two known misconceptions: calling a career high-demand because it sounds popular, and pasting a national salary where the sheet asks for DFW. If more than a handful of students are missing the growth percentage at lap 2, pivot and reproject the BLS Job Outlook tab to the whole room instead of repeating the search desk by desk.
- **Chunking:** Day 2, Activity 1, the four stages of the apprenticeship pipeline. Distinguished: teach one stage at a time on the projected infographic, say what each stage is for before the numbers go up (Apply is the door, Apprentice is paid training, Journeyman is independent work, Master is the license to run a crew and a business), and close each stage with a quick check before releasing the next. Note which stage draws the most confusion. That stage becomes a 3 minute re-teach at the start of Day 4's pathway mapping, and students who cleared all four stages start the union research early instead of waiting. The Day 5 jigsaw is chunked the same way, one trade per team.
- **Time, Voice, Body (TVB):** Day 5, Activity 1, the jigsaw trade presentations. Distinguished: post the three blocks before the first team stands up, Voice 0 while a team gets its notes set, Voice 3 for the presenter, Voice 1 for the note-taking sheet between teams, and run a visible 3 minute countdown for every team so no team gets more air time than another. Define where presenters stand and where the note-taking sheets go so transitions do not eat presentation time. Narrate the teams meeting the expectation rather than correcting the ones who are not. If the rotation is running long by the third team, tell the remaining teams to lead with the salary range and the outlook and announce the change before the next team begins.

## Differentiation

### Scaffolded Learning
- Pre-filled BLS data for one construction career as a worked example on the Classification worksheet
- Visual apprenticeship pipeline infographic (apprentice → journeyman → master) for students who need a structural reference
- CareerOneStop Compare Occupations tool pre-loaded on classroom computers
- Inspection report sentence starters for students who need scaffolding: "In Image #__ I saw _____. This matters because _____."

### Extensions
- Powerskill: Creativity mosaic mural (FYF pp. 191-193): design the downtown mural on graph paper where each square is one 6-inch tile, then plan the install order with a partner
- Research the DFW construction labor shortage with specific hiring data from contractors
- Compare union vs. non-union wages in construction trades
- Calculate lifetime earnings: apprenticeship entry at 18 vs. college entry at 22
- Interview a local contractor or tradesperson about their career path

### ELL Language Support
- Pre-teach: Construction = Construcción, Apprenticeship = Aprendizaje, Trade Union = Sindicato, Journeyman = Oficial, Master = Maestro
- Visual apprenticeship infographic that is accessible across languages
- Bilingual Classification worksheet with Spanish column headers
- Pair ELL students with bilingual peers during the Jigsaw research
- Inspection vocabulary previewed before the images go up: Crack = Grieta, Leak = Fuga, Mold = Moho, Wiring = Cableado, Insulation = Aislamiento, Roof = Techo. The five inspection photos carry the evidence visually, so students can record what they see before they have the English word for it
