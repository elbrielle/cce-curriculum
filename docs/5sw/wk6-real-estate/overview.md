# Week 6: Location, Location, Location — Real Estate Careers

**5th Six Weeks** | Business, Marketing & Finance Cluster | 5 class periods (50 min each)

## Lesson Objective

Students explore real estate careers through the Hats & Ladders Business, Marketing, and Finance cluster and the *Find Your Future* workbook; research Texas Real Estate Commission (TREC) licensing requirements; compare commission-based income to salary-based income; run the "Flip This House" renovation-budget activity to see how investors weigh what an upgrade costs against what it adds; and analyze the DFW real estate market as a career destination.

**Learning Target:** I can name three real estate careers and the license each one needs, explain how commission pay is different from salary pay, and use DFW market data to explain why real estate work is growing here.

**Success Criteria:** I will know I am successful when my career research sheet lists the license, the income model, and the DFW salary range for one real estate career, when my commission worksheet shows the math for four home prices, when my renovation plan stays under the $25,000 budget and gives a buyer reason for every upgrade I picked, and when my 2-minute pitch uses at least two numbers I found this week.

## Demonstration of Learning

> "I can describe at least three real estate careers with their licensing requirements, explain the difference between commission-based and salary-based income, and analyze why the DFW real estate market creates strong career opportunities."

## Career Connection

Real estate is a career that blends **sales skills**, **market knowledge**, and **entrepreneurship**. In the DFW metroplex, one of the fastest-growing real estate markets in the country, agents, brokers, appraisers, and property managers have extraordinary opportunities. What makes real estate unique is the **income model**: most real estate agents work on commission, meaning they earn a percentage of each sale rather than a fixed salary. Income is directly tied to effort and skill.

Real estate also has a strong **entrepreneurship connection:** most agents are independent contractors who essentially run their own business. Texas requires a TREC (Texas Real Estate Commission) license, which students can begin earning right out of high school through specific coursework.

**What is Happening at Irving ISD?** **Real Estate Marketing at MacArthur High School** sits inside MacArthur's **School of Business and Entrepreneurship**, alongside Administrative Management, Entrepreneurship, and Sales Management. The workbook's district pages name Real Estate as one of the district's six Business, Marketing, and Finance programs of study and describe it as preparing students for careers in property valuation, contract law, and business communication (FYF pp. 252-253: "What is Happening at My District?"). The same pages carry the local detail students respond to most: real estate students have worked alongside real estate professionals to learn property evaluation, contract law, and the local housing market; Business and Marketing students run school stores on their own campuses and sell products they designed themselves; the cluster's industry-based certification is Entrepreneurship and Small Business; and its student organizations are DECA and Business Professionals of America. Students can start the coursework in high school and sit for the Texas Real Estate Sales Agent exam once they turn 18. MacArthur's program certifications poster lists Real Estate Sales Agent as this pathway's credential, but neither the workbook's district pages nor the campus CTE page names it, so treat the certificate itself as **[VERIFY with CTE coordinator]** before telling students the pathway ends there.

## Vocabulary

- **Real Estate Agent:** A licensed professional who helps people buy, sell, or rent properties. Agents must pass a state licensing exam.
- **TREC:** Texas Real Estate Commission, the state agency that regulates real estate licensing in Texas.
- **Commission:** A payment based on a percentage of a sale. Real estate agents typically earn 2.5-3% of a home's sale price per transaction.
- **Broker:** A real estate professional with additional training and licensing who can manage other agents and run a brokerage firm.
- **Appraiser:** A licensed professional who determines the market value of a property.
- **Entrepreneurship:** Starting and running your own business. Real estate is the most common path to entrepreneurship in this cluster.

## Bridge to Theory (Hats & Ladders)

The *Find Your Future* workbook gives Business, Marketing, and Finance printed pages 221-254. The chapter opener (FYF p. 221: "Exploring the World of BUSINESS, MARKETING, AND FINANCE") frames the cluster as helping organizations grow, connect with customers, and make smart money decisions, puts a "Be the Decision Maker" question in front of students (a local business can afford only one move this year: a major advertising campaign, more employees, or new equipment), and introduces three Hats on the page: Business Analyst, Financial Examiner, and E-Commerce Specialist. The chapter prints no pathway list. Students meet the cluster's pathways in the Hats & Ladders app through the Pathway Possibilities step on the App Exploration page (FYF p. 254: "App Exploration"), and they meet Irving ISD's own six Business programs of study, Real Estate among them, on the district pages (FYF pp. 252-253: "What is Happening at My District?").

Two chapter activities carry this week:

- **Flip This House** (Day 3): Career Climb activity. Pairs are a real estate investment team that just bought a fixer-upper in a growing town. They read what buyers in that town actually want, spend a $25,000 renovation budget from a table of eight upgrades that each carry a cost and an estimated value increase, and lock in a written renovation strategy that justifies every choice (FYF pp. 238-240: "Flip This House", Steps 1-3). This is where return on investment stops being a vocabulary word. Steps 4 and 5, writing the listing and sharing the flip with a small group, run as the Day 3 extension.
- **Powerskill: Visual Storytelling** (Day 5 extension): partners work as the social media marketing team for a listed property. They table the home's strengths and challenges by category (Interior, Outdoor Space, Neighborhood, Location), design three to five posts that tell one story rather than list features, and write the full listing with a strong hook and a call to action (FYF pp. 231-233: "Powerskill: Visual Storytelling").

Students also explore real estate Hats in the app using the Hat Finder, pulling salary, licensing, and demand data, and then apply the entrepreneurship standards from earlier in the year to recognize that real estate is one of the most common entrepreneurship paths.

The rest of this chapter is taught in other weeks and referenced here, not adopted: Million Dollar Idea (FYF pp. 234-237) ran in 3SW Wk6, Expert Edge and Click Factor sit in 6SW Wk3, and the 30-second sales pitch (FYF pp. 241-243) sits in 6SW Wk4.

## IISD Instructional Strategies

- **Think-Pair-Share:** Day 1, Activity 2, the four-station Gallery Walk of real estate roles and the DOK 2 question that closes it. Distinguished: hold the question until every pair has all four cards, then run a silent 30 second Think, 30 seconds for Partner A and 30 seconds for Partner B, with two stems posted at different levels: "Commission pay is different from salary pay because _____" and "I would rather be paid _____ because a household that needs _____ can plan around it." Ask two pairs to share, not one. Listen during the pair talk for who names the risk as well as the upside, and pick the share-out pairs from what you heard rather than from raised hands.
- **Stop and Jot:** Day 3, the Warm-Up quick write on being your own boss. Distinguished: put 90 seconds on the clock, require one reason yes and one reason no in writing before any talk, and post two stems: "Being my own boss would be good because _____" and "The part that would worry me is _____." Read jots over shoulders while students write and track what the worries name. If most of them name money that does not arrive on the same day every month, open Flip This House by naming that exact risk, because the $25,000 budget decision is the same worry in miniature.
- **Active Monitoring:** Day 3, Activity 1, the Flip This House upgrade choices. Distinguished: walk a fixed pathway twice. Lap 1 target: every pair's circled upgrades total $25,000 or less. Lap 2 target: the written renovation strategy names a buyer preference from Step 1 as the reason for each upgrade. Have feedback ready before class for the two predictable errors, a team that spends most of the budget on the luxury closet and the designer lighting because those sound impressive, and a team that adds up the value increases without subtracting the costs. If more than a handful of pairs miss the lap 1 target, stop the room and rework one line of the upgrade table on the projector instead of fixing it pair by pair.
- **Chunking:** Day 2, Activity 2, the four sections of the Commission vs. Salary Comparison worksheet. Distinguished: model one calculation on the projector before Section 1 (a $350,000 home at 3 percent is $10,500, and the split between the buyer's agent and the seller's agent leaves $5,250), then release one section at a time and check before the next: per-sale math, annual income at three sales volumes, the comparison to a $70,000 salary, then the written reflection. Note which section draws the most hands. If it is Section 2, reteach the multiply-then-split order to the whole room before students write Section 4, and let pairs who cleared Sections 1 and 2 start the Broker licensing extension early.
- **Time, Voice, Body (TVB):** Day 5, Activity 1, the 2-minute career pitches. Distinguished: post the three blocks before the first presenter, Voice 1 for the five minutes of partner practice, Voice 0 while a presenter sets up, Voice 3 for the presenter, and run the visible 2 minute countdown already written into the plan. Say where the presenter stands and where written pitches go for the students who chose that option. Narrate the students who are meeting the expectation rather than correcting the ones who are not. If the rotation is running long, announce before the next presenter that the remaining pitches move to partner delivery so every student still delivers one.

## Differentiation

### Scaffolded Learning
- Commission calculation examples with step-by-step math worked out for students who need support
- Simplified TREC licensing research guide with specific website pages pre-bookmarked
- Allow students to complete the elevator pitch in written form rather than presenting orally
- Pre-filled Gallery Walk notes for one career

### Extensions
- "Powerskill: Visual Storytelling" (FYF pp. 231-233) as the Day 5 extension: partners assess the workbook's listed property and its Oakridge neighborhood, table the strengths and challenges by category, design three to five social posts that tell one story, and write the full listing with a hook and a call to action
- Research the difference between a real estate agent and a real estate investor (flipping homes, rental properties, REITs)
- Calculate total commission earned over a 20-year real estate career with increasing sales volume and home prices
- Compare TREC licensing requirements to real estate licensing in California or New York, how do they differ?
- Research current DFW average home prices by neighborhood and identify which zip codes have the highest commission potential

### ELL Language Support
- Pre-teach: Real Estate = Bienes raíces, Commission = Comisión, License = Licencia, Appraiser = Tasador, Broker = Corredor
- Commission math worksheets use numbers and formulas that are language-accessible
- Bilingual TREC research guide with Spanish headers
- Pair ELL students with bilingual peers during the elevator pitch activity
