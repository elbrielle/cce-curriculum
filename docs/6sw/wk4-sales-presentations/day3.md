## Warm-Up (5 min)

**WARM-UP: Think of an ad you have seen recently that did NOT work on you. What made you scroll past it, skip it, or ignore it?**

Take 3 student responses. Bridge to today: a campaign can reach exactly the right people and still fail. Today students inherit one that did, and their job is to find out why.

## Activity 1: Powerskill Problem Solving — BrainBoost Bars Campaign Rescue (25 min)

**Source:** (FYF pp. 244-247: "Powerskill: Problem Solving")

Set the frame the workbook uses: problem solving means spotting the challenge, figuring out what is causing it, and building a plan that works. Some problems take a few tries and some creative thinking, and people who are good at it stay calm, handle mistakes, and make smart choices.

Groups of 3-4 take over a campaign for BrainBoost Bars, a healthy snack bar for busy students and young adults. The campaign reached exactly the intended audience, students aged 14 to 18 who are active and focused on school and extracurriculars. Engagement and sales still came in low.

**Step 1: Analyze the Campaign (6 min)**

Groups review the three campaign pieces printed on pp. 244-245, the email, the social media post, and the in-store display, against the workbook's three questions: Is the messaging clear and interesting? Do the visuals show why the product is special? Is anything confusing or boring?

The workbook also prints three lines of real customer feedback, and they are the strongest clue in the activity:

- "Looks like any other snack."
- "I'm not sure why I would try this."
- "This doesn't seem like something I would need."

Each group writes ONE sentence naming why the campaign might be underperforming. That sentence is the problem statement, and everything after it has to answer it.

**Step 2: Brainstorm Solutions (9 min)**

**Stop and Jot:** One idea per sticky note, at least ten notes per group, no discussion and no filtering for the first four minutes. Post the workbook's four idea lanes so no group stalls on one: improve the messaging or the slogan, add more exciting visuals or scenarios, offer a promotion or an incentive, or change the social media and email content to fit the audience. Read notes over shoulders as they go up. If a group's notes are all slogan rewrites, name a lane they have not touched and keep walking rather than solving it for them. Groups then pick their best three ideas and write how each one improves engagement.

**Step 3: Create a Mini Campaign Plan (7 min)**

Each group designs the new version: sketch or outline the social post, the email copy, or the in-store display, then decide on a clear message and a unique selling point. The unique selling point is the direct answer to "looks like any other snack," so groups that skip it have not fixed the problem they wrote down.

**Step 4: Share and Reflect (3 min)**

Groups pair up and present the redesign to one other group. Each listening group names one thing the presenting group noticed that they had missed.

!!! tip "Facilitation Tip"
    The most common wrong turn is a group that redesigns the visuals and never changes the message. Point them back at their own problem statement and ask which of the three customer comments their redesign answers. If they cannot name one, the redesign is decoration.

**DOK 4:** Of the redesigns you saw today, which one would get the most engagement from students aged 14 to 18? Use specific evidence from the customer feedback and from the group's plan.

## Activity 2: Individual Career Presentation — Outline (18 min)

**Source:** Scope and sequence d(4)(C): oral professional presentation + (FYF p. 299: "Prepare & Present")

Transition to the major individual deliverable for the week. Each student will give a 3-minute oral presentation on Friday (Day 5) on a career they have explored this year.

Distribute the Career Presentation Outline template and the Career Presentation Rubric.

**Chunking:** Teach the structure one part at a time and say what each part is for before modeling it. Deliver a 30 second teacher model of each part on the projector using a career the class has already studied, then close each part with a check before releasing the next one.

**Required structure (3 minutes total):**

1. **Introduction (30 seconds).** This part earns attention.
    - Career name
    - Why you chose this career to present (personal connection)
2. **Body. Three Key Facts (2 minutes).** This part earns trust.
    - **Fact 1:** Education path required (high school → college / trade school / certification)
    - **Fact 2:** DFW salary (entry, median, top 10%)
    - **Fact 3:** Daily tasks, what does this person actually DO every day?
3. **Conclusion (30 seconds).** This part asks the audience to decide with you.
    - Connection to your personal Career Plan
    - One sentence: *"This career fits me because _____"* OR *"I learned this career is NOT for me because _____"*

Both conclusions are valid. The point is reflection, not enthusiasm.

Walk the rubric's delivery criterion at the same time, using the four levels the workbook prints for **Presenter Delivery**: engaging and confident, clear delivery, some difficulty, hard to follow (FYF p. 280). Students should know before they write what the top level sounds like.

**Student work time (15 min):**

Students choose their career (from any cluster they explored this year. Manufacturing, IT, Law, Health Science, Ag, Hospitality, Human Services, Business, A&C, Engineering, Transportation, Education, Arts/AV, Marketing, Sales). They write the outline on the template.

The outline must be COMPLETE before students leave class. Day 4 is for practice, not for catch-up.

Close Activity 2 with a direct student-facing announcement: Friday's presentations are cut at 3:00, and Thursday's paired practice exists so students can time themselves and adjust. Students need to hear the rule today, not on Friday.

!!! tip "Facilitation Tip"
    Watch for students choosing a career they have not actually researched. Push back: "What is the salary? What's the daily work?" If they don't know, send them to H&L Hat Finder or BLS to gather data right now. The presentation cannot be built on guesses.

**DOK 3:** What conclusions can you draw about why a presentation needs an introduction, body, and conclusion? Why not just list the facts?

**DELIVERABLE:** Team problem statement, three chosen solutions, and mini campaign plan + individual Career Presentation Outline complete.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a partially-filled outline template with the structure pre-labeled and example phrases. Allow students to choose from a list of 6 well-researched careers (Marketing Manager, Graphic Designer, Sales Manager, Teacher, Architect, IT Specialist) if they cannot decide. For the campaign rescue, give the group a problem statement stem: "The campaign did not work because customers could not tell _____."
- **Extension:** Students draft a 1-slide visual aid (Canva or Google Slides) to accompany the presentation, a single slide showing the career name + salary + one image.
- **ELL:** Provide a bilingual outline template with Spanish sentence starters ("Mi carrera es _____. Elegí esta carrera porque _____"). Allow the presentation to be delivered in Spanish or bilingual format. Pair ESL students with bilingual peers for outline review and for the sticky-note brainstorm, where drawings count as ideas.
