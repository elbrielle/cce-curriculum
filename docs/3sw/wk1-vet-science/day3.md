# Day 3: Powerskill: Critical Thinking — Veterinary Triage

## Lesson Overview

| | |
|---|---|
| **Time** | 50 minutes |
| **Objectives** | Work the triage desk at an emergency animal hospital; compare two patients' vitals against normal-range references; identify the critical information in each chart; decide who is seen first and defend the decision with evidence |
| **TEKS** | d(1)(C) |
| **5E Phases** | Engage: Warm-Up and triage scenario · Explore: Patient charts and normal-range check · Explain: Share and compare decisions · Evaluate: Exit Ticket |
| **Deliverable** | Completed triage notes for both patients (critical information for Leo and Barnaby) + a written decision naming who is seen first and the evidence behind it |
| **Materials** | *Find Your Future* workbook pp. 96-99; projector; optional printed copies of the two patient charts and the normal-range references for students who need them off-screen |

---

## Warm-Up (5 min)

**WARM-UP: Two sick animals arrive at an animal hospital at the exact same second. Only one vet is free. How would you decide who gets seen first?**

Take 3 fast responses and write the deciding factor from each one on the board (loudest, biggest, sickest, first through the door). Leave the list up. Today students find out that the real answer is none of those, and that the job of sorting patients by urgency has a name.

---

## Activity 1: The Triage Desk + Meet the Patients (10 min)

**Source:** (FYF pp. 96-97: "Powerskill: Critical Thinking"), Step 1

Introduce the Powerskill first. The workbook defines critical thinking as the ability to analyze information and make thoughtful decisions. Strong critical thinkers ask questions, look closely at details, and use evidence to guide their choices. Point at the warm-up list on the board and note which of those factors are guesses and which are evidence.

Set the scenario (FYF p. 96). Students are a veterinary technician working the triage desk at a busy emergency animal hospital. Triage is the critical first step when a sick or injured animal arrives. Today two very different patients arrive at the exact same time, and the technician has to decide who needs emergency care first.

**Patient Chart #1: Leo (FYF p. 96).** Leo is a 3-year-old ball python. The owner reports that Leo has not eaten recently, has been hiding, and hissed when handled, and notes dull skin and cloudy eyes. The family recently moved to a new apartment and added a new water bowl last week. Triage assessment: weight 2.64 pounds, behavior alert and defensive and coiled tightly, heart rate 34 bpm. Physical observations: eyes are opaque and milky blue with complete loss of clarity, skin across the whole body is dull and dry with slight wrinkling near the neck, inside of the mouth is normal color, no external parasites or wounds.

**Patient Chart #2: Barnaby (FYF p. 97).** Barnaby is a 4-year-old Golden Retriever. The owner reports that he began pacing and whining about an hour after eating, with abdominal swelling, and has been retching repeatedly with no productive vomit, producing only white foamy saliva. Triage assessment: weight 74.96 pounds, behavior highly anxious and pacing and unable to get comfortable, heart rate 150 bpm with a pulse that feels weak and rapid. Physical observations: abdomen visibly distended, firm, and painful on gentle touch.

Students read both charts and underline every number and every physical observation. No decision yet.

!!! tip "Facilitation Tip"
    Most students pick Leo on first read, because cloudy eyes and dull skin look alarming and a snake is unfamiliar. Do not correct that yet. The whole point of Step 2 is that a symptom cannot be judged until it is measured against what is normal for that animal, and the reversal lands harder if students commit to a first impression before they get the reference pages.

## Activity 2: Normal Ranges, Critical Information, Decision (25 min)

**Source:** (FYF pp. 98-99: "Powerskill: Critical Thinking"), Steps 2-4

**Step 2: Check the Normal Ranges (8 min).** Students turn to the reference page (FYF p. 98) and ask one question about every stat they underlined: is this normal or abnormal? The workbook supplies both reference sets.

| Reference | Ball python (adult) | Canine, Golden Retriever (adult male) |
|---|---|---|
| Weight | 1,000 to 1,500 grams (2.2 to 3.3 lbs) | 65 to 75 lbs |
| Heart rate / pulse | 25 to 40 bpm, steady | 60 to 100 bpm, strong and steady |
| Behavior | Calm, slow-moving, tolerates gentle handling | Alert, relaxed, able to settle comfortably |
| Physical findings | Eyes clear and glossy, skin smooth and vibrant | Abdomen soft, flat, not painful when touched gently |

Both reference boxes carry an "Important note" that decides the case, and students need to read both before they go on. The ball python note explains that a python preparing to shed will refuse food, hide, become irritable, and develop dull skin and cloudy eyes, a normal biological process called ecdysis. The canine note explains that in large, deep-chested breeds, bloating with unproductive retching after eating can point to a serious internal condition, and that unlike a regular stomach upset this kind of situation can become life-threatening quickly.

**Step 3: Identify Critical Information (10 min).** Students write the critical information for each patient in the two columns on FYF p. 99. **Stop and Jot:** 5 minutes per patient, silent, with two stems on the screen: "This finding is outside the normal range because _____" and "This finding looks bad but the reference page says it is normal because _____." Every line students write has to name the reference number or the note it came from.

**Step 4: Make a Decision (7 min).** Each student answers the workbook question on p. 99: who needs to be seen first, and why? The justification has to cite at least two pieces of evidence from the charts or the reference page.

!!! tip "Facilitation Tip"
    Walk a fixed pathway three times, one target per lap. Lap 1, every student has compared Barnaby's 150 bpm against the 60 to 100 reference. Lap 2, every student has found the ecdysis note and marked Leo's cloudy eyes and dull skin as expected rather than alarming. Lap 3, every decision sentence carries two pieces of evidence. Two misconceptions are worth having an answer ready for before class: "the snake looks worse" (send them back to the reference line, not the photo) and "the dog is not bleeding so he can wait" (send them to the note about deep-chested breeds). If more than a handful of students miss the Lap 2 target, pause the room and reread the ecdysis note together instead of repeating it desk by desk.

**DOK 3:** What conclusions can you draw about why a triage technician has to know the normal range for every species that walks in, instead of trusting how sick a patient looks?

## Activity 3: Share and Compare (8 min)

**Source:** (FYF p. 99: "Powerskill: Critical Thinking"), Step 5 and Class Discussion

Students join a small group and compare decisions using the workbook's own prompts: did every group member choose the same patient to be seen first, and did they use the same evidence to get there? Groups that agree on the patient but disagree on the evidence are the interesting ones, so ask those groups to report both routes.

Close with two of the workbook's class-discussion questions (FYF p. 99): why is critical thinking a Powerskill in a high-stress workplace like an emergency animal hospital, and what is the difference between a symptom that looks scary and a symptom that is actually dangerous? Then name the team: a real clinic runs this decision through a Veterinarian, a Vet Tech, an Animal Care Assistant, and a Practice Manager, and the triage technician's call sets the order everyone else works in.

!!! note "What the Evidence Should Show"
    Barnaby is seen first. His heart rate of 150 bpm is well above the 60 to 100 canine range, his pulse is weak and rapid, and his abdomen is distended, firm, and painful, which the reference page ties to a condition that can turn life-threatening quickly. Every one of Leo's findings sits inside the ball python reference ranges (2.64 lbs, 34 bpm) or is explained by the shedding note. Leo still gets an appointment, just not the emergency slot.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a triage note-catcher with the four reference rows pre-printed and a blank NORMAL / ABNORMAL box beside each finding, so students sort before they write. Sentence stems: "Barnaby's heart rate is _____ and the normal range is _____, so it is _____." and "Leo's cloudy eyes are _____ because the reference page says _____."
- **Extension:** Write Patient Chart #3 for a third animal arriving at the same time, with a full triage assessment and at least one finding that looks alarming but is normal for that species. Trade with a partner and see whether they can spot the fake alarm.
- **ELL:** Pre-teach: Triage = Triaje, Symptom = Síntoma, Heart rate = Frecuencia cardíaca, Swollen = Hinchado, Normal range = Rango normal, Evidence = Evidencia. The two charts read well above grade level, so read them aloud while students underline, and let students write their critical-information notes in Spanish or bilingually.
