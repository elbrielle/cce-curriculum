# Week 6: Be Your Own Boss — Entrepreneurship

**3rd Six Weeks** | Business, Marketing & Finance Cluster | 5 class periods (50 min each)

## Lesson Objective

Students explore the Business, Marketing, and Finance cluster through the Hats & Ladders app and the *Find Your Future* workbook, define entrepreneurship in their own words, and work the full "Million Dollar Idea" entrepreneurial process (FYF pp. 234-237): spot a problem worth solving, generate at least 10 business ideas in a timed sprint, screen the list to two, stress-test both, and make the call to move the strongest idea forward, keep working on it, or abandon it. Groups then pitch their chosen venture to a classmate investor panel, and students close the six weeks by building a personal budget on a real DFW career salary. **This is the strongest d(3)(I) week of the entire year.**

**Learning Target:** I can find a problem worth solving, turn it into business ideas, test my two best ideas, decide which one is worth my time, pitch it to a group, and build a budget on a real career salary.

**Success Criteria:** I will know I am successful when my cross-cluster list names a business and one business skill for five different clusters, when my idea sprint page holds at least 10 ideas and my top 2 each have a reason, when my strongest idea carries a written call of Move Forward, Needs Work, or Abandon It with its biggest strength and biggest risk, when my group's venture brief fills all six sections and I speak one of them in the 2-minute pitch, and when my personal budget totals no more than my monthly take-home pay.

## Demonstration of Learning

> "I can define entrepreneurship in my own words, find a problem worth solving and generate at least 10 business ideas from it, test my two strongest ideas and decide whether to move one forward, pitch my group's venture to a class investor panel, build a personal budget that reflects a future career salary, and identify entrepreneurial opportunities across at least 3 career clusters."

## TEKS Alignment

- **d(3)(I):** Define entrepreneurship and identify entrepreneurial opportunities within a field of personal interest (Days 1-5). **(PRIMARY, strongest d(3)(I) week of the year)**
- **d(4)(F):** Define and identify examples of work ethic, integrity, dedication, and perseverance (Day 4, the pitch preparation reflection).

## Vocabulary

- **Entrepreneur:** A person who creates, organizes, and manages a business, taking on financial risk in hopes of making a profit.
- **Problem Statement:** One or two sentences naming what is going wrong and why it matters. Every idea this week starts from one.
- **Rapid Idea Generation:** Writing as many ideas as possible in a short, timed burst without stopping to judge, fix, or erase any of them.
- **Validation:** Testing an idea before building it. Does it solve the problem, would someone choose it over other options, and what would make it hard to build?
- **Investor:** A person or company that puts money into a business with the hope of earning more money in return.
- **Pitch:** A short, persuasive presentation that explains a business idea and asks the listener to back it.
- **Profit:** Revenue minus expenses. The money a business owner keeps after paying all costs.
- **Personal Budget:** A plan for managing income and expenses over a specific time period (usually monthly).
- **Work Ethic:** A set of values centered on the importance of hard work, reliability, and dedication.

## Bridge to Theory (Hats & Ladders)

The *Find Your Future* workbook gives Business, Marketing, and Finance printed pages 221 to 254. The chapter opener (FYF p. 221: "Exploring the World of BUSINESS, MARKETING, AND FINANCE") frames the cluster as helping organizations grow, connect with customers, and make smart financial decisions, puts a "Be the Decision Maker" spending dilemma in front of students, and introduces three Hats on the page: Business Analyst, Financial Examiner, and E-Commerce Specialist. The chapter does not print a pathway list. Students meet the cluster's pathways in the Hats & Ladders app through Pathway Possibilities on the App Exploration page (FYF p. 254), and they meet Irving ISD's own programs of study on the district pages (FYF pp. 252-253).

One chapter activity carries this week:

- **Million Dollar Idea** (Days 2-3): Career Climb activity, and the workbook's longest, with 55 lines of student writing across four pages. Students spot a problem they care about, generate at least 10 to 12 ideas in a five-minute sprint without judging any of them, screen the list to their top 2, stress-test both against three entrepreneur questions, and then make the call: Move Forward, Needs Work, or Abandon It. The activity closes with a small-group pitch where each group picks the one idea with the most potential (FYF pp. 234-237: "Million Dollar Idea").

The rest of this chapter is taught in later weeks and is referenced here, not adopted: real-estate investing and property marketing sit in 5SW Wk6, consulting and advertising sit in 6SW Wk3, and the 30-second sales pitch (FYF pp. 241-243) sits in 6SW Wk4. Day 4's investor panel is a CCE-authored structure, not a workbook activity; every line of the venture brief students pitch traces back to their own Million Dollar Idea pages.

## IISD Instructional Strategies

- **Think-Pair-Share:** Day 1, Activity 1, the "Be the Decision Maker" prompt on the chapter opener (FYF p. 221), where a local business can afford exactly one of three moves this year: an advertising campaign, more employees, or new equipment. Distinguished: pose it to the whole group, hold a silent 30 second Think, then time Partner A for 30 seconds and Partner B for 30 seconds, and post two stems at different levels: "I would pick _____ because _____" and "Hiring more people helps the business _____, but new equipment helps it _____." Ask two pairs to share, not one. Listen while pairs talk for who names what information they would need before deciding, and pick your share-out pairs from what you heard rather than from raised hands.
- **Stop and Jot:** Day 1, Activity 2, the two pauses during the Business cluster tour video on the App Exploration page (FYF p. 254). Distinguished: name both stop points before the video starts, give 45 seconds per jot with the prompt on screen, and post two stems: "One business career I did not know existed is _____" and "One question I still have about starting a business is _____." Keep a third stem on the board for the cross-cluster list in Activity 3: "An entrepreneurial opportunity in _____ is _____, and it needs _____ work ethic because _____." Read jots over shoulders during both pauses. If the jots name only store owners and YouTubers, steer the Hat browse toward Business Analyst, Financial Examiner, and E-Commerce Specialist so Activity 3's list is not five versions of the same business.
- **Active Monitoring:** Day 2, Activity 2, the five-minute idea sprint and the top-2 screen that follows. Distinguished: walk two laps with one target each, lap 1 at minute 3 of the sprint checking that every page carries at least six ideas and nobody is erasing, lap 2 during Step 3 checking that each "why this works" sentence answers one of the workbook's three screening questions instead of restating the idea. Have the response ready before class for the two predictable misconceptions: the student who stops at four ideas because they only want good ones, and the student who picks the first idea on the list without screening it. If more than a handful of pages stall under six ideas, pause the room, model the low bar by putting three of your own throwaway ideas on the board, and restart the clock for the time that remains.
- **Chunking:** Day 3, Activity 1, the three stress-test questions in Step 4 (FYF p. 236). Distinguished: release one question at a time rather than all three, say what each one is for before students write (question 1 tests the fit, question 2 tests the competition, question 3 tests the build), give roughly two minutes per question across both ideas, and take the thumbs check between questions before releasing the next. Note which question draws the most thumbs down. That question becomes a 3 minute re-teach before Step 5, and students who cleared all three start drafting their Make the Call reasoning early instead of waiting.
- **Time, Voice, Body (TVB):** Day 4, Activity 2, the venture pitches to the investor panel. Distinguished: post the three blocks before the first group stands, Voice 0 while a group sets up, Voice 3 for whoever is speaking, and Voice 1 for the star and wish writing between pitches, with a visible 2 minute countdown on every pitch. Name the one spot on the front desk where finished feedback slips go, and hold the rule that every group member speaks one section of the brief. Narrate the groups that are meeting the expectation rather than correcting the ones that are not. If the rotation is running long by the third group, cut the panel question to a single panelist and announce the change before the next group begins.

## Formative Assessment

- Cross-cluster entrepreneurship list + Mini-Case exit ticket (cousin with $3,000 picks a cluster, a business, and a business skill). *Day 1, d(3)(I)*
- Problem statement, sprint idea count, and top 2 with reasons + Short Constructed Response exit ticket (strongest idea, one dropped idea and the screening reason it failed). *Day 2, d(3)(I)*
- Stress-test notes on both ideas + written call + Comparison Matrix exit ticket (two ideas across the three tests, then the row that decided it). *Day 3, d(3)(I)*
- Venture brief + 2-minute pitch + peer feedback + Trade-off Dilemma exit ticket (polish the brief vs. rehearse the group member; work-ethic quality pick). *Day 4, d(3)(I), d(4)(F)*
- Personal budget + Xello saves + Concept Map exit ticket (career + biggest budget line item + entrepreneurial version + self-as-entrepreneur). *Day 5, d(3)(I), d(5)(D)*

## Summative Assessment

**Entrepreneurship Portfolio (3rd Six Weeks Capstone):** Students submit (1) their completed "Million Dollar Idea" pages from Days 2-3 (FYF pp. 234-237: problem statement, idea list, top 2 with reasons, stress-test notes, and the written call), (2) the group venture brief and the peer feedback forms from Day 4, (3) the completed personal budget from Day 5, and (4) a one-paragraph reflection answering: "Do I see myself as an entrepreneur? Why or why not?" Scored on entrepreneurship definition and application across clusters (d(3)(I)), budget accuracy (d(5)(D)), work ethic reflection (d(4)(F)), and personal career self-awareness. A written call of Abandon It earns full credit when the reasoning names a real strength and a real risk; the verdict is not what is being scored.

## Differentiation

### Scaffolded Learning
- Problem menu with six school-sized problems already written (lunch line, lost water bottles, phone charging, bus wait, homework tracking, recycling) so no student loses the Day 2 sprint choosing a problem
- Idea sheet with 10 numbered blanks so the sprint target is visible on the page
- Stress-test grid with the three Step 4 questions across the top and the two ideas down the side
- Venture brief template with the six section headers and a sentence stem under each
- Personal budget worksheet with DFW cost-of-living data already entered

### Extensions
- Take the idea your group did NOT choose and name the one change that would have made it the pick, then price that change in money, time, or difficulty
- Rewrite your group's venture brief as a 30-second version. Which two sections did you cut, and what did the pitch lose?
- Research a real young entrepreneur (BizKids profile or local Irving small business owner) and present findings
- Build a full year-1 plan for the group's venture: projected costs, projected income, and the month it would break even

### ELL Language Support
- Pre-teach: Entrepreneur = Emprendedor/a, Business = Negocio, Problem = Problema, Risk = Riesgo, Investor = Inversor/a, Budget = Presupuesto, Profit = Ganancia, Pitch = Presentación
- Bilingual venture brief and budget templates with Spanish section labels
- Ideas may be written in Spanish, English, or both during the Day 2 sprint; speed matters more than language while the timer runs
- The venture pitch can be delivered in English, Spanish, or bilingual, many real Texas business pitches are bilingual
- Pair with bilingual peers during pitch prep
