# Day 1: Powerskill: Analytical Reasoning — Diagnose the Truck

## Lesson Overview

| | |
|---|---|
| **Time** | 50 minutes |
| **Objectives** | Explain what analytical reasoning is and where it gets used; diagnose four truck warning-light issues from their clue sets; rank the four by urgency and justify which two to fix first |
| **TEKS** | d(4)(B) |
| **5E Phases** | Engage: Warm-Up · Explain: Code reader and dashboard lights · Explore: Partner diagnosis of four issues · Explain: Urgency ranking and class discussion · Evaluate: Exit Ticket |
| **Deliverable** | Completed diagnosis chart (possible problem + explanation for all four issues) + urgency ranking + the two issues chosen to fix first with reasoning |

## Warm-Up (5 min)

**WARM-UP: A car makes a noise today that it did not make yesterday. Name three clues you would check before you guess what is broken.**

Take 4-5 student responses. Surface the clue types students name on their own: what the noise sounds like, when it happens, warning lights, smells, how the car feels to drive. Bridge: that list is the job. A mechanic does not start with the repair, they start with the clues. Today's Powerskill gives that habit a name and puts a truck with four warning lights in front of the room.

---

## Activity 1: Understand Your Tools (10 min)

**Source:** (FYF p. 153: "Powerskill: Analytical Reasoning", Step 1), the Transportation chapter's Powerskill lesson

Open the workbook to page 153 and read the opening together. Analytical reasoning is the skill of looking at clues and working out what they mean. It is noticing details, asking questions, and making a smart call based on evidence. People use it any time they solve a problem, make a decision, or figure out what is happening.

Then read the scene. Students are an auto mechanic in a busy garage. A truck has come in with warning lights on the dashboard. Their job is to look for clues and figure out what is wrong with it.

Step 1 gives students two tools:

| Tool | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| **Code Reader** | A small tool that plugs into the truck and shows a short code hinting at which part of the truck might have a problem |
| **Dashboard Lights** | Colored lights that turn on when something needs attention |

Name the limit of each tool before students touch the issues. A code points at a system, not at a repair. A light says something is wrong, not how bad it is. Everything between the tool and the decision is the reasoning, and that is what students are graded on today.

---

## Activity 2: Diagnose the Problems (20 min)

**Source:** (FYF p. 154: "Powerskill: Analytical Reasoning", Step 2)

Students pair up. The workbook prints four issues, each with its own clue set:

| Issue | Clues |
|---|---|
| **Issue 1** | Oil light on, code for engine oil, oil looks dark, engine feels hotter than usual |
| **Issue 2** | Battery light on, code for the battery, truck struggles to start, interior lights are dim |
| **Issue 3** | Temperature light on, code for engine temperature, engine very hot, steam from under the hood, radiator fan running constantly |
| **Issue 4** | Tire pressure light on, code for tire pressure, tires look slightly flat, truck pulls to one side, tires feel soft when pressed |

For each issue, pairs fill in two columns: **Possible Problem** and **Explanation**. The workbook directs students to research to confirm, so Chromebooks come out for the confirmation step only, after a pair has written their first guess from the clues.

**Think-Pair-Share:** Read Issue 1's clue set to the whole room. Hold a silent 30 second Think, then time Partner A for 30 seconds and Partner B for 30 seconds. Two pairs share before anyone moves on. Post two stems: "The clue that matters most is _____ because _____" and "Issue _____ and issue _____ could be connected because _____." Pairs then work Issues 2 through 4 on their own.

Walk a fixed pathway twice while pairs work. Lap 1 target: a possible problem written for all four issues. Lap 2 target: every explanation names at least one clue from the table. Have feedback ready for the two predictable errors, copying a clue straight into the Possible Problem column, and writing "needs a mechanic" four times. If more than a handful of pairs miss the lap 2 target, reproject Issue 1 and model one explanation for the whole room instead of fixing it pair by pair.

!!! tip "Facilitation Tip"
    Students reach for a part name they have heard of, usually "the engine is broken," instead of reasoning from the clues. Push them back to the set: dark oil on its own is a different story from dark oil plus an engine running hot. The reasoning is the deliverable here, not the right part name.

---

## Activity 3: Prioritize the Issues + Make a Plan (12 min)

**Source:** (FYF p. 155: "Powerskill: Analytical Reasoning", Steps 3-4)

**Step 3: Prioritize.** Pairs rank all four issues using the workbook's three levels:

1. **Critical / Fix Immediately:** safety is compromised, or driving will cause severe damage
2. **High Priority / Plan Soon:** not an immediate threat, but it should be fixed soon
3. **Monitor / Can Wait:** the vehicle works, and regular inspections should continue

They then answer the workbook's reasoning prompts: how did you decide which issues were most urgent, could any of the problems be connected, and what might happen if a problem is not fixed quickly?

!!! tip "Facilitation Tip"
    The printed ranking grid on p. 155 labels its fourth box "Issue 3" a second time. It is Issue 4. Say so before students start ranking, or part of the class will rank three issues and stop.

**Step 4: Make a plan.** There is only time to fix two of the four. Pairs pick their top two and write the reasoning behind the pick, working from which issues the clues make most urgent and how fixing one might affect another.

Close with the workbook's class discussion: which strategies helped you reason through the problems, how can analytical reasoning help with big decisions and with small ones, and when is analytical reasoning easiest to use versus hardest? Run the middle question deliberately and push each response to name a career or a situation outside a garage. That question is the bridge into the Transferable Skills Matrix on Day 2.

**DOK 3:** Two of the four warning lights could come from the same underlying problem. Which two, and what evidence in the clue sets supports connecting them?

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Pre-filled diagnosis chart with Issue 1's possible problem and explanation written in as a model, so students copy the reasoning pattern into Issues 2 through 4. Pair with a peer for the research step. Students may rank only their top two issues instead of all four.
- **Extension:** Write the customer call. In four sentences, tell the truck's owner which two issues you are fixing today, what each one is, and what happens if the other two are ignored for a month.
- **ELL:** Bilingual clue table with Spanish column labels. Pre-teach: Clue = Pista, Evidence = Evidencia, Urgent = Urgente, Warning light = Luz de advertencia, Repair = Reparación, Pressure = Presión. Allow the Possible Problem and Explanation columns to be written in Spanish. The clue sets are short phrases, so the table itself reads across language levels.
