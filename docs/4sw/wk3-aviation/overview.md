# Week 3: Cleared for Takeoff — Aviation Careers

**4th Six Weeks** | Transportation, Distribution & Logistics Cluster | 5 class periods (50 min each)

## Lesson Objective

Students explore the Transportation, Distribution, and Logistics cluster through the Hats & Ladders app and the *Find Your Future* workbook, complete the "Transportation Survey Project" activity to think like a Transportation Needs Analyst, research aviation careers (pilot, air traffic controller, aircraft mechanic, drone operator), compare military and civilian pathways into aviation, and run a LEGO air traffic control simulation that models real ATC communication and spatial reasoning.

**Learning Target:** I can name at least three aviation careers and the training each one needs, compare the military and civilian ways into aviation, and run clear air traffic control calls in our LEGO airport simulation.

**Success Criteria:** I will know I am successful when my survey has 7 multiple-choice questions, 3 short-answer questions, and one incentive, when my career research sheet lists the job, the training, the pay, and the tools for one aviation career, when all three pathway columns on my comparison chart are filled in, and when my team's Simulation Run Log shows three runs and names one fix we made between runs.

## Demonstration of Learning

> "I can describe at least three aviation careers with their education requirements, complete the Transportation Survey Project, compare military and civilian pathways into aviation, and demonstrate ATC communication through a LEGO simulation."

## Career Connection

Aviation careers combine math, physics, communication, and split-second decision making. They are also unusually accessible because there are multiple legitimate entry pathways: 4-year college aviation programs, FAA-certified flight schools, and military service through the Air Force, Navy, Army, or Coast Guard. The DFW airspace is one of the busiest in the country, which means commercial pilot, air traffic controller, and aviation maintenance jobs are concentrated locally.

**What is Happening at Irving ISD?** **Aviation Maintenance** and **Drone Engineering** at **Irving High School** (School of Aviation Science) are the district's two aviation programs of study. The workbook's district pages carry the detail students respond to most (FYF pp. 168-169: "What is Happening at My District?"): students in the Aviation program at Irving High School train on **two full-motion flight simulators**; the Aviation Maintenance program of study prepares students for careers in aircraft maintenance systems, inspection, repair, and safety procedures; SkillsUSA is the student organization named for this cluster; and the same pages describe Automotive Enterprise Days, the community events where student mechanics service vehicles at heavily discounted rates. Students can also enter aviation through Marine JROTC at Irving High School or through military service.

> [VERIFY] The workbook's district pages name Automotive Service Excellence as the industry-based certification for this cluster, which is the automotive credential rather than an aviation one, while PATHWAYS.md lists Industrial Technology Maintenance (ITM) Electrical System for Aviation Maintenance and FAA Part 107 for Drone Engineering. Confirm with your CTE coordinator which certification an Aviation Maintenance student actually earns before advising a student toward the pathway.

## Vocabulary

- **Air Traffic Controller (ATC):** Directs aircraft on the ground and in the air to keep planes safely separated.
- **FAA (Federal Aviation Administration):** US agency that regulates aviation and issues pilot, ATC, and drone certifications.
- **JROTC:** Junior Reserve Officers' Training Corps. A high school program that introduces students to military careers and leadership.
- **Part 107:** The FAA certification required to operate drones for any commercial purpose.
- **Minimum Equipment List (MEL):** The list aircraft mechanics use to decide whether a plane can still fly with a known problem. It is the go or no-go call in the Flight Line Fixers extension.
- **Military Service Pathway:** A career entry route through the armed forces that provides paid training, job experience, and credentials transferable to civilian careers.
- **Transportation Needs Analyst:** A professional who studies how a community moves and recommends transit improvements. This is the career anchor for the Transportation Survey Project.

## Bridge to Theory (Hats & Ladders)

The *Find Your Future* workbook gives Transportation, Distribution, and Logistics printed pages 149-170. The chapter opener (FYF p. 149: "Exploring the World of TRANSPORTATION, DISTRIBUTION, AND LOGISTICS") frames the cluster as moving people and products from place to place efficiently and safely, puts a "Be the Decision Maker" shipping dilemma in front of students (split a 48-hour national shipment across several transportation methods, or streamline one), and shows three Hats on the page: Automotive Engineering Technician, Autobody Technician, and Heavy Truck Driver. The chapter does not print a pathway list. Students meet the cluster's pathways in the Hats & Ladders app through the Pathway Possibilities step on the App Exploration page (FYF p. 170: "App Exploration"), and they meet Irving ISD's own aviation program of study on the district pages (FYF pp. 168-169).

Two chapter sections carry this week:

- **Transportation Survey Project** (Day 1): Career Climb activity. Students are the new Transportation Needs Analyst for their community after town meetings full of complaints about public transportation. They discuss local transportation challenges on sticky notes, develop a 10-question survey with 7 multiple-choice and 3 short-answer questions, design an incentive that motivates people to respond, and build a campaign that recruits participants (FYF pp. 166-167: "Transportation Survey Project").
- **Flight Line Fixers** (Day 2 extension): Career Climb activity, and the only aviation activity in the workbook. Students are the aircraft mechanic examining a commercial airplane that has just landed. They study a common-aircraft-issues chart, inspect the teacher's aircraft photos with sticky notes, complete an Inspection Report, rate the issue on a Low, Medium, or High Severity Guide, make a Minimum Equipment List go or no-go call, and write a Maintenance Plan naming at least three tools before checking their decision against a partner's (FYF pp. 160-165: "Flight Line Fixers"). It needs the teacher's Climber Notes deck for the aircraft photos.

## IISD Instructional Strategies

- **Think-Pair-Share:** Day 1, Activity 2, Step 1, the sticky-note discussion of transportation challenges in Irving (FYF p. 166). Distinguished: pose the question to the whole group, hold a silent 30 second Think, then time Partner A for 30 seconds and Partner B for 30 seconds before the group pools its sticky notes, and post two stems at different levels: "One way people in Irving have trouble getting around is _____" and "A person who _____ needs a different kind of transportation than a person who _____, because _____." Ask two pairs to share, not one. Listen while pairs talk for who names a specific rider (a night-shift worker, a student without a car) rather than a general complaint, and pick your share-out pairs from what you heard rather than from raised hands.
- **Stop and Jot:** Day 1, Activity 1, the two pauses during the Transportation cluster tour video. Distinguished: name both stop points before the video starts, give 45 seconds per jot with the prompt on screen, and offer two stems: "One career in this cluster I did not expect is _____" and "One thing I still want to know about _____ is _____." Read jots over shoulders during both pauses and track which careers get named. If most jots say pilot only, steer the Hat browse that follows toward Aircraft Mechanic, Flight Dispatcher, and Air Traffic Controller so Day 2's research sheets are not thirty copies of the same career.
- **Active Monitoring:** Day 4, Activity 1, the three ATC simulation runs. Distinguished: walk a fixed pathway once per run with one target per lap (Run 1, the controller names the runway in every clearance; Run 2, the pilot reads the command back before moving; Run 3, the team clears the runway before the emergency landing), carrying the communication clipboard already built for this week. Have feedback ready before class for the two known breakdowns: a clearance with no runway number, and two pilots moving at once because nobody acknowledged. If more than a handful of teams miss the Run 2 target, pivot and model one full call-and-readback to the whole room instead of stopping team by team.
- **Chunking:** Day 3, the three-part build sequence, Activity 1 constraints, Activity 2 sketch and peer critique, Activity 3 LEGO build. Distinguished: release one chunk at a time and say what each one is for before students start it (the constraints define the problem, the sketch tests the layout on paper where changes are free, the build makes it real), and close each chunk with the check already written into the plan: the sketch is approved before bricks are handed out, and the five-row spatial checklist is walked before a team is finished. Note which checklist row fails most often. That row becomes a two minute re-teach at the start of Day 4, and teams that clear all five rows early read their Day 4 scenario card instead of waiting.
- **Time, Voice, Body (TVB):** Day 5, Activity 1, the 3 minute team ATC presentations. Distinguished: post the three blocks before the first team presents, Voice 0 while a team carries its airport to the front, Voice 3 for the presenter, Voice 1 for the listening grid, and run a visible 3 minute countdown for every team. Define where airports sit while teams wait and where the listening grids go when they are done, so transitions do not eat presentation time. Narrate the teams that are meeting the expectation rather than correcting the ones that are not. If the rotation is running long by the third team, cut the demonstration to one radio call and announce the change before the next team starts.

## Differentiation

### Scaffolded Learning
- Pre-built LEGO airport base for teams that need a starting point (1 runway already laid out)
- Simplified ATC scenario cards (Tier 1: 2 planes; Tier 4: 6 planes plus weather and emergencies)
- Pre-printed Military vs. Civilian comparison chart with the categories filled in (students only fill in the data)
- Sentence frame for the survey: "How often do you _____?" and "Do you have access to _____?"
- Reduced career research, one aviation career instead of two

### Extensions
- **Flight Line Fixers** (FYF pp. 160-165): students who finish the Day 2 research early inspect the Climber Notes aircraft photos, complete the workbook Inspection Report, apply the Severity Guide, and make the Minimum Equipment List go or no-go call with a partner
- Research the specific Air Force or Navy pilot training pipeline; build a timeline from enlistment to pilot certification
- Add weather, emergency landings, or runway closure scenarios to the ATC simulation
- Investigate the Irving High Drone Engineering pathway and map the courses from 9th to 12th grade

### ELL Language Support
- Pre-teach: Air Traffic Controller = Controlador de tráfico aéreo, Pilot = Piloto, Mechanic = Mecánico, Military = Militar, Civilian = Civil, Aviation = Aviación
- ATC simulation uses verbal commands that are valuable for academic English practice, provide a bilingual command card (Cleared for takeoff = Despeje para despegar; Hold position = Mantenga posición; Taxi to gate = Taxi a la puerta)
- LEGO building is hands-on and visually accessible across language levels
- Pair ESL students with bilingual peers during team builds
- The workbook reads well above grade level, so read the survey scenario aloud and encourage browser translation for any independent reading
