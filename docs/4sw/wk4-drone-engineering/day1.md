## Warm-Up (5 min)

**WARM-UP: Name three things you have seen a drone do, in person, on TV, or online.**

Take 5-6 student responses on the board: wedding photography, racing, package delivery, sports filming, surveillance, or crop spraying. Ask what would have to change for a drone to track an endangered animal in dense forest. Students will design for that need today.

## Activity 1: H&L Engineering Cluster Tour (12 min)

**Source:** (FYF p. 103: "Exploring the World of ENGINEERING") + (FYF p. 110: "App Exploration") + (FYF pp. 108-109: "What is Happening at My District?")

Open the workbook to page 103 and read the opener together. Engineering is about designing, building, and improving the world around us, and engineers, technicians, and systems designers work together to solve problems and develop new ideas. Three Hats sit on that page: Aerospace Engineer, Biomedical Engineer, and CAD Technician.

> [H&L PLATFORM] The App Exploration page walks students through the cluster in order: open the Hats & Ladders app, go to Clusters, click the Engineering Cluster, watch the "Cluster Tour" video, play "Game Time," find 1 Hat that matches their personality or interests and 1 Hat that does not fit, open "Pathway Possibilities" and rate at least 1 pathway, then rate at least 3 Hats. Students record their answers on page 110. **Stop and Jot:** pause the video once, 45 seconds, using the stem "One engineering career I did not expect in this cluster is _____."

The chapter prints no pathway list, so the pathways come from the app's Pathway Possibilities step. Point students toward drone and unmanned-systems work while they rate.

Irving ISD names the pathway directly. The workbook's district pages describe a **Drone Engineering** program of study that prepares students for careers in unmanned aircraft systems (UAS), aviation, and drone engineering through flight operations, design, programming, maintenance, and data collection, and they list **FAA Part 107** among the cluster's industry-based certifications alongside Autodesk Fusion 360 and Autodesk CAD Inventor (FYF pp. 108-109: "What is Happening at My District?"). That program of study is the confirmation that drones are engineering work with a credential behind them, not a hobby. It is the source grounding for the rest of the week.

After the app exploration, students browse the Hat Finder for 3-4 engineering Hats. Start with the three on the chapter opener, Aerospace Engineer, Biomedical Engineer, and CAD Technician, then add Drone Engineer, Mechanical Engineer, Robotics Technician, Electrical Engineer, or Civil Engineer.

!!! tip "Facilitation Tip"
    Some students will want to spend 20 minutes browsing Hats. Set a 10-minute timer and stop the room when it goes off. The cluster tour is the warm-up, not the main event today.

## Activity 2: H&L "Protecting Wildlife" Robot Blueprint (28 min)

**Source:** (FYF pp. 104-105: "Protecting Wildlife"), a Career Climb activity

Open the workbook to page 104 and read the framing together. Engineers work with people from many other career fields, including business owners, sports teams, and wildlife conservationists. Conservationists work hard to study and protect wildlife, but they often run into challenges that make it difficult to follow animals and gather important data. That is where engineers come in.

**The student role:** A Robotics Engineer, which means someone who uses technical skills to design and build robots. A wildlife conservationist has come to you with a challenge. They need a small, lightweight robot that can fly into dense forests and track animals, and they are particularly interested in tracking **jaguars**.

**Step 1: Learn the Requirements (3 min)**

Read the requirement list on page 104 with students. The robot must:

- Be small and lightweight
- Fly into dense forests and navigate around trees
- Collect and send data during the day and at night of animals eating, sleeping, or playing
- Show exactly where an animal is located in the forest
- Record videos and sound

The page also gives three field conditions that make the job hard: rainforests are very humid with unpredictable weather, jaguars are most active at night, and jaguars swim and hunt fish, turtles, and small alligators. Every one of those is a design constraint.

**Step 2: Brainstorm Ideas (3 min)**

Students think about what their robot will look like and write ideas on sticky notes, which is what the workbook asks for on page 104. Useful prompts while they work: how will it fly, rotors or wings or glide-and-perch? How will it see at night, infrared or low-light cameras? How will it find the animal, GPS tags or sound? How will data get back, radio, cellular, or satellite?

**Step 3: Design Your Robot (16 min)**

Page 105 asks students to sketch a blueprint of the robot and label the key features. Distribute the printed Blueprint sheet, students sketch in pencil first, then label in pen.

The blueprint must include labels for at least:
- **Flight system** (rotors, wings, propellers, how it stays in the air)
- **Power source** (battery, solar, fuel)
- **Navigation system** (cameras, sensors, GPS, how it avoids trees)
- **Data collection equipment** (cameras, microphones, GPS tags, how it tracks animals)
- **Communication antenna** (how data gets back to researchers)
- **Bonus:** Stealth features so animals don't notice the drone (camo paint, quiet motors)

Students who draw a generic quadcopter without labels will need a redirect, the point of the blueprint is the labeled components, not the artistic quality.

!!! tip "Facilitation Tip"
    Project a real conservation drone image (search "wildlife conservation drone", many examples on FAA and NPS sites). Show students that real conservation drones look like military quadcopters with cameras hanging underneath. They do not need to invent a new shape. They need to think about which sensors and communication systems matter for tracking specific animals.

**Step 4: Share Your Design (6 min)**

Page 105 asks students to pair up or join a small group, share the design, and discuss what is similar and what is different between them. **Think-Pair-Share:** run the workbook's first Class Discussion question, what could go wrong if the design of the robot was not tested before it was used in the field, as 30 seconds of silent Think, then Partner A for 30 seconds, then Partner B for 30 seconds. Two pairs share with the whole class.

Close with the other two Class Discussion questions from page 105: which of your Building Blocks would give you an advantage in this role, and why might someone choose this career instead of designing robots for entertainment, sports, or business?

This activity is the bridge from "drones in general" to "drones used by engineers for specific purposes." It sets up Day 2's industry research.

**DOK 2:** How would you describe the connection between a wildlife conservationist's needs and a robotics engineer's design choices? Why does the engineer need to understand the conservationist's job?

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Pre-printed blueprint with the rotors and battery already drawn. Students add navigation, data collection, and communication labels. Pair with a partner who can act as the conservationist explaining what they need.
- **Extension:** The workbook's "Extra Time?" box on page 105 asks students to name their robot and list at least 2 other professions that could benefit from the design. Students who finish that add a power management plan: how long can the robot fly before recharging, and what does the conservationist do when it needs to return?
- **ELL:** Bilingual blueprint label sheet. Pre-teach: Drone = Dron, Engineer = Ingeniero, Wildlife = Vida silvestre, Tracker = Rastreador, Camera = Cámara, Battery = Batería, Sensor = Sensor.
