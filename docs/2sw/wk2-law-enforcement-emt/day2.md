# Day 2: Clinton Lake Case — Weigh the Evidence

## Lesson Overview

| | |
|---|---|
| **Time** | 50 minutes |
| **Objectives** | Choose a side in a civil case; review an evidence packet and rate each piece strong, weak, or unclear; build an argument from the three strongest pieces and defend it against the opposing side |
| **TEKS** | d(1)(C), d(4)(F) |
| **5E Phases** | Engage: Warm-Up · Explore: Evidence packet review · Explain: Build your case and Face-Off Discussion · Evaluate: Exit Ticket |
| **Deliverable** | Completed case build (side chosen, main argument, three strongest pieces of evidence, response to the opposing side, proposed solution) |
| **Materials** | *Find Your Future* workbook pp. 48-49, Climber Notes deck "Clinton Lake Case" (slides 2-7), sticky notes, Chromebooks, projector |

---

## Warm-Up (5 min)

**WARM-UP: A lake in your town turns green, starts to smell, and swimmers come out with itchy rashes. What is the FIRST question you would ask to find out what happened?**

Take 3-4 responses. Look for "What is in the water?" or "What is upstream?" Both are evidence questions, which is exactly where today starts. Bridge: the workbook's chapter opener says this cluster mixes people who respond in the moment with people who investigate, analyze, and piece together what happened. Yesterday was the response side. Today students take the investigation side and argue a real dispute over that lake.

---

## Activity 1: Clinton Lake Case Setup + Choose Your Side (10 min)

**Source:** (FYF p. 48: "Clinton Lake Case"), a Career Climb activity

Open the workbook to page 48. Read the opening paragraph together: lawyers help solve problems using rules, evidence, and reasoning. Some defend people who are accused of doing something wrong, and others defend people who believe they have been harmed. Lawyers do not decide who is good or bad. Their job is to build the strongest argument for the side they represent, which means looking at evidence carefully, asking tough questions, and thinking critically before making a case.

Then read the case. Clinton Lake was once a popular summer hangout. The water has turned murky green, it smells bad, swimmers are getting itchy rashes, and wildlife in the area is affected. Two groups are pointing fingers:

- **The Citizens** believe a cracked wall at the City's landfill is leaking toxic waste into the lake.
- **The City** believes a storm of the century caused the damage, and that illegal dumping by citizens is what actually polluted the lake.

Both sides disagree strongly and both have evidence.

**Step 1: Choose Your Side (p. 48).** Each student circles Citizens or City in the workbook. Students must defend that side using evidence and reasoning, whatever they personally believe happened. Aim for a rough split in the room so the Face-Off in Activity 3 works; if one side is badly outnumbered, ask a few volunteers to switch and tell them why lawyers get assigned to a side rather than picking one.

!!! tip "Facilitation Tip"
    Some students will resist arguing a side they do not agree with. That resistance is the lesson. Point back at the opening paragraph: a lawyer's job is to build the strongest argument for the side they represent, and the judge or jury decides who is right. Students who still want to argue the other side can do it in the extension.

## Activity 2: Investigate the Evidence (20 min)

**Source:** (FYF p. 48: "Clinton Lake Case", Step 2) and (Climber Notes: "Clinton Lake Case", slides 2-7)

The evidence packet is in the teacher's Climber Notes deck, not in the student workbook. Project the six files one at a time and give students time on each before moving on:

1. Lake Water Report
2. Landfill Inspection Note
3. Weather Report: Storm of the Century
4. City Statement
5. Environmental Investigation Note
6. Wildlife Impact Report

**Stop and Jot:** Students write on one sticky note per file, using the workbook's own three questions (p. 48): what does the evidence show, does it support my side or the opposing side, and is it strong, weak, or unclear. Give 60 seconds per file with the three questions posted on the screen. Two stems for students who stall: "This file shows _____" and "This is strong or weak evidence because _____."

Students stick the notes in two columns on their desk or notebook page: helps my side, helps their side. That sort is what Activity 3 builds from.

!!! tip "Facilitation Tip"
    Read the room as you circulate. If most sticky notes are rating the weather report as strong evidence for the City, pull the class back for 30 seconds and ask what that report does not say about the condition of the landfill wall. Evidence that is silent on the key question is not strong evidence, and that distinction is the whole activity.

**DOK 4:** What conclusions can you draw about why both sides can look at the same six files and still disagree? Could a case like this end with both sides partly responsible? Use one specific file to support your answer.

## Activity 3: Build Your Case + Face-Off Discussion (12 min)

**Source:** (FYF p. 49: "Clinton Lake Case", Steps 3-4)

**Step 3: Build Your Case (7 min).** Students use the workbook space on page 49 to answer four questions:

- What is my main argument? Who is responsible, and why?
- What are my three strongest pieces of evidence?
- How would I respond to the opposing side?
- Solution: what should be done to fix the problem?

The workbook prints one sample statement per side as a model of the length and tone expected: the Citizens' lawyer argues the City failed to maintain the landfill and that the cracked retaining wall shows clear negligence; the City's lawyer argues a rare and severe storm caused unavoidable damage and that illegal chemical dumping by citizens is the real source. Students write their own; the samples show the level of detail, not the answer.

**Step 4: Face-Off Discussion (5 min).** Each student pairs with someone from the opposite side.

Project these three norms and read them before pairs start:

1. **Lead with evidence, not volume.** Start every claim with "My strongest evidence is ___" or "File ___ shows ___."
2. **Attack the case, not the person.** "Your evidence is weaker because ___" is fair. "You're wrong" is not.
3. **Name one thing the other case gets right.** Before the pair ends, each partner says "One strong point in your case is ___."

**Think-Pair-Share:** 30 seconds of silent Think on the workbook's closing question, whether lawyers should focus more on winning or on finding the truth. Then Partner A presents for 90 seconds and Partner B presents for 90 seconds, as the workbook asks. Each partner then asks one question or challenges one point. Two pairs share out, chosen from what you heard while circulating rather than from raised hands.

## Exit Ticket (3 min)

**EXIT TICKET** (Diagnostic MCQ with Misconception Distractors) · [Printable PDF](../../resources/exit-tickets/2sw-wk2-day2-clinton-lake-case-weigh-the-evidence.pdf):

A lawyer for the City finds one file that makes her own side look bad. No one else has seen it yet. She has to decide what to do with it.

To handle this the right way, which quality does the lawyer need MOST?

- A. Speed, because cases have to be finished fast.
- B. Honesty, because hiding evidence that hurts your side is not fair and not allowed.
- C. Confidence, because good lawyers never doubt their own case.
- D. Luck, because most cases are decided by chance.

Circle your answer. In one sentence, say why the other three choices are weaker. *(d(1)(C), d(4)(F))*

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide an evidence tracker, a six-row table with one row per file and three columns (What it shows / Helps which side / Strong, weak, or unclear). This scaffolds the sticky-note sort into a form students can hand in. Sentence stem for Step 3: "The _____ is responsible because _____, and my strongest proof is _____."
- **Extension:** Students argue the case from the side they did not choose. Which of their original three pieces of evidence still holds up, and which one flips?
- **ELL:** Bilingual evidence tracker with the six file names translated. Pre-teach: Evidence = Evidencia, Lawyer = Abogado, Landfill = Vertedero, Toxic waste = Desechos tóxicos, Storm = Tormenta, Wildlife = Vida silvestre, Responsible = Responsable. Pair ESL students with bilingual peers. The evidence files are projected images with short text, so they are accessible across language levels, and the workbook reads well above grade level, so read the case paragraphs aloud.
