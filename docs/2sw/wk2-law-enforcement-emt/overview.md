# Week 2: First Responders — Protecting Our Community

**2nd Six Weeks** | Law, Public Safety, Corrections & Security Cluster | 5 class periods (50 min each)

## Lesson Objective

Students explore law enforcement, EMT, firefighter, and search-and-rescue careers through the Hats & Ladders app and the *Find Your Future* workbook, research and compare the entry pathways first responders use (academy, certification, military), build a side of the "Clinton Lake Case" from an evidence packet, and work the "Injured on the Trail" wilderness rescue as a Wilderness First Responder, stabilizing a hiker and documenting the care in a Patient Care Report. Students connect the cluster to Irving ISD's Criminal Justice and Emergency Medical Technician programs of study.

**Learning Target:** I can name the entry path and training time for at least three first responder careers, build a case from the three strongest pieces of evidence, treat an injured hiker and write up the care I gave, and explain why honesty matters in public safety work.

**Success Criteria:** I will know I am successful when my Training Comparison worksheet lists the entry path, training time, credential, and DFW salary for all five careers, when my Clinton Lake case names my side and my three strongest pieces of evidence, when my partner's sling and finger splint are in place and my Patient Care Report has all five sections filled in, and when my reflection names one moment this week where integrity mattered.

## Demonstration of Learning

> "I can describe the training pathways for at least three first responder careers, build an argument from the strongest pieces of evidence in a case, stabilize an injured hiker as a Wilderness First Responder and document that care in a Patient Care Report, and explain why integrity and perseverance are non-negotiable for first responders."

## Career Connection

Police officers, EMTs, paramedics, and firefighters respond when public safety is at risk. The work calls for physical fitness, quick thinking, emotional resilience, and integrity. Entry routes include military service (MP, Master-at-Arms, Navy Corpsman, Army medic, Air Force Security Forces), police academies, and short EMT certification programs as well as college. Some students can graduate high school and begin full-time work in the field within 12 months. **JROTC** at Irving ISD high schools introduces the military route into first responder work without committing a student to military service.

**What is Happening at Irving ISD?** Both of this week's pathways live at Singley Academy on the application-based magnet campus:

- **Law Enforcement** (School of Law and Public Service) leads to the Non-Commissioned Security Officer Level II certification, which prepares students for entry-level security and pre-academy work.
- **Emergency Medical - EMT** (School of Health Science) leads to the Emergency Medical Training (EMT-B) certification, the same entry credential used by working EMTs in DFW ambulance services.

The Law and Public Safety district pages describe the **Criminal Justice** program of study, which covers how laws are developed and enforced, the organization of local, state, and federal law enforcement, criminal law, investigation, and legal responses to violations (FYF pp. 56-57: "What is Happening at My District?"). The sidebar names the security officer certification and **Explorers**, one of the cluster's student organizations, with a short explanation of CTSOs and their competitions and projects. The Health Science district pages cover the EMT side: the **Emergency Medical Technician (EMT)** program of study teaches emergency response, patient assessment, trauma care, and life-saving techniques, and EMT students at Singley ride with emergency medical teams on real calls (FYF pp. 84-85: "What is Happening at My District?").

Students interested in the Firefighter pathway typically enter through a post-high-school fire academy; Irving ISD does not currently offer a Fire Science pathway, so cover that role as career exploration using BLS and local fire-department resources.

## Vocabulary

- **EMT (Emergency Medical Technician):** A healthcare professional trained to provide emergency medical care and transport patients. Entry cert is 6-12 weeks.
- **Paramedic:** An advanced EMT who can administer medications, perform advanced procedures, and lead a medical response team. Requires 1-2 years of additional training.
- **Police Academy:** An intensive training program (typically 6-8 months) that prepares recruits to become law enforcement officers.
- **First Responder:** The first trained professional to arrive at the scene of an emergency, including police, firefighters, and EMTs.
- **Search and Rescue (SAR):** Teams that respond to emergencies in remote places like mountains, forests, and deserts, using skill, strategy, and teamwork to reach people who need help.
- **Wilderness First Responder (WFR):** A specially trained medic on a SAR team who provides medical care where hospitals are far away. The main goal is to stabilize the patient, which means keeping an injury from getting worse and protecting the patient until they reach advanced care.
- **Patient Care Report (PCR):** The written record a responder completes after a call, describing the scene, the patient's condition, the care provided, and the reasoning behind each treatment.
- **Integrity:** Doing the right thing even when no one is watching. Critical for first responders trusted with public safety.
- **Perseverance:** Continuing to work toward a goal despite challenges. Essential for the physical and emotional demands of emergency services.

## Bridge to Theory (Hats & Ladders)

The *Find Your Future* workbook gives Law and Public Safety printed pages 39-58, and the chapter frames the cluster as a mix of fast action and careful decision-making: some roles respond in the moment during emergencies, while others investigate, analyze, and piece together what happened (FYF p. 39: "Exploring the World of LAW AND PUBLIC SAFETY"). Week 1 of this cluster works the policy and courtroom side through "City Council in Action" (FYF pp. 40-43), "Policy Showdown" (FYF pp. 44-47), and "Emergency Essentials: Kit Design" (FYF pp. 50-51). Week 2 takes the investigation and response side.

The chapter does not print a pathway list. Students meet the cluster's pathways in the Hats & Ladders app through the Pathway Possibilities step on the App Exploration page (FYF p. 58: "App Exploration"), and they meet Irving ISD's own programs of study on the district pages (FYF pp. 56-57).

This week uses two chapter activities, and both need the teacher's Climber Notes deck:

- **Clinton Lake Case** (Day 2): Career Climb activity. Clinton Lake has turned murky green, it smells, swimmers are getting rashes, and wildlife is affected. Citizens blame a cracked wall at the city landfill; the city blames a "storm of the century" plus illegal dumping. Students choose a side, work through the evidence packet with sticky notes, rate each piece strong, weak, or unclear, build an argument from their three strongest pieces, and face off with a partner from the opposing side (FYF pp. 48-49: "Clinton Lake Case"). The six evidence files live in the Climber Notes deck (Climber Notes: "Clinton Lake Case", slides 2-7): a lake water report, a landfill inspection note, a weather report, a city statement, an environmental investigation note, and a wildlife impact report.
- **Injured on the Trail** (Days 3-4): Career Climb activity. A hiker slipped on loose gravel on the steep Granite Ridge trail and cannot continue, with a shoulder that looks out of place and a swollen, bent finger. Students research Search and Rescue and Wilderness First Responder work, take turns as responder and injured hiker while applying a sling and a finger splint, write a Patient Care Report narrative, and then improvise through a wilderness complication (FYF pp. 52-55: "Injured on the Trail"). The supply reference table and the two technique photos are in the Climber Notes deck (Climber Notes: "Injured on the Trail", slides 2-3).

The day-to-day career research format is the CCE career research worksheet taught in Wk0. This week it takes the shape of the First Responder Training Comparison, built on Day 1 and finished on Day 5.

## IISD Instructional Strategies

- **Think-Pair-Share:** Day 2, Activity 3, the Clinton Lake "Face-Off Discussion" where each student pairs with someone from the opposite side. Distinguished: hold a silent 30 second Think on the workbook's own question, whether lawyers should focus more on winning or on finding the truth, then time Partner A for 90 seconds and Partner B for 90 seconds before the challenge round. Post two stems at different levels: "My strongest evidence is _____ because _____" and "Your case is weaker at _____, and the evidence that shows it is _____." Ask two pairs to share, not one. Listen while pairs talk for who names a piece of evidence that hurts their own side, and pick your share-out pairs from what you heard rather than from raised hands.
- **Stop and Jot:** Day 2, Activity 2, the sticky-note pass through the six evidence files. Distinguished: name the three-part prompt before the first file goes up (what does this show, does it help my side or theirs, is it strong, weak, or unclear), give 60 seconds per file with the prompt on screen, and offer two stems: "This file shows _____" and "This is strong or weak evidence because _____." Read jots over shoulders as you circulate and track which files students are calling strong. If most sticky notes rate the weather report as strong for the city, pull the class back for 30 seconds and ask what the report does not say about the landfill wall. The same routine lands again on Day 5 during the two Roadtrip Nation video pauses, where students capture one surprising fact about the career path and one trait the responder shows.
- **Active Monitoring:** Day 3, Activity 3, the two Wilderness First Responder rounds. Distinguished: walk a fixed pathway twice, one lap per round, each lap with a single target (round 1, the sling supports the arm and limits shoulder movement; round 2, the finger splint keeps the finger straight and the cohesive wrap holds it without cutting off circulation), marking a clipboard as you go and noting who is doing the work in each pair rather than watching. Have the feedback ready before class for the two known misconceptions: a wrap pulled so tight the fingertip changes color, and a sling knotted so low that the forearm hangs below the elbow. If more than a handful of pairs miss the round 1 target, pivot and reproject the Climber Notes technique photo to the whole room instead of fixing it pair by pair.
- **Chunking:** Day 3, Activity 2, the sling-then-splint demonstration. Distinguished: teach one skill at a time from the Climber Notes supply table, say what each supply is for before demonstrating it (the triangular bandage supports the arm, the cohesive wrap holds a splint in place, the finger splint keeps the finger straight), and close each chunk with a thumbs check before releasing the next. Note which skill drew the most thumbs down; that skill becomes a two minute re-teach at the start of Activity 3, and pairs who cleared both chunks start their scene notes early instead of waiting. Day 1, Activity 2 is chunked the same way: the Training Comparison worksheet is five careers across five columns, and students fill one or two careers at a time with a checkpoint between.
- **Time, Voice, Body (TVB):** Day 4, Activity 3, the group-to-group complication briefings. Distinguished: post the three blocks before the first briefing, Voice 0 while the receiving group reads the plan, Voice 2 for the briefing itself, Voice 1 for the question round, and run a visible countdown for every rotation. Define exactly where the sling, wrap, and splint go between rounds so supplies do not disappear into backpacks. Narrate the groups meeting the expectation rather than correcting the ones who are not. If the first rotation runs long, cut the question round to one question per group and announce the change before the next rotation starts.

## Summative Assessment

**Injured on the Trail Patient Care Report + Complication Briefing (Day 4):** Each student submits a Patient Care Report and each group briefs another group on its wilderness complication plan (FYF pp. 53-54: "Injured on the Trail", Steps 4-5). The report must include: (1) a scene description naming where the patient was and what the environment was like, (2) the patient's condition and the injuries observed, (3) the care provided, describing how the sling and the finger splint were used, (4) the reasoning behind each treatment, supported by the Step 1 research, and (5) how those actions prepared the patient for safe transport. The briefing must name the complication chosen, explain the reasoning for each step, and show how the decisions keep both the patient and the team safe. The receiving group owes the briefing group one question or one alternative approach, which is the workbook's own Step 5 requirement. Scored with the **Daily Performance & Career Skills Rubric** from the H&L teacher resources: Work Ethic & Effort, Productivity & Progress, Problem-Solving & Thinking, and Communication & Collaboration. Aligned to d(1)(C) and d(4)(F).

## Differentiation

### Scaffolded Learning

- Pre-filled First Responder Training Comparison with one row (EMT) modeled
- Clinton Lake evidence tracker with a guided question stem for each file: "This file helps the _____ because _____"
- Pre-assigned partner pairings for the Injured on the Trail rounds so students who struggle with negotiation can focus on the technique
- Sentence stems for the integrity reflection

### Extensions

- Compare military first responder pathways (Combat Medic, Military Police) with civilian pathways. Where do they overlap?
- Research a real first responder you know (school resource officer, family member) and create a 1-page profile
- Argue the Clinton Lake Case from the side you did not choose. Which of your original three pieces of evidence still holds up?
- Build a 4-year plan for the Singley Academy Criminal Justice program of study including dual-credit options

### ELL Language Support

- Pre-teach: Police Officer = Oficial de Policía, EMT = Técnico de Emergencias Médicas, Firefighter = Bombero, Evidence = Evidencia, Sling = Cabestrillo, Splint = Férula, Rescue = Rescate, Integrity = Integridad
- Bilingual Clinton Lake evidence tracker with the six file names translated
- Visual supply cards with icons for the sling, the cohesive wrap, and the finger splint
- Pair ESL students with bilingual peers during partner activities. The evidence files and the Climber Notes technique photos are visual and accessible for all language levels, and the workbook reads well above grade level, so read the scenario paragraphs aloud
