# Day 3: Injured on the Trail — Stabilize the Patient

## Lesson Overview

| | |
|---|---|
| **Time** | 50 minutes |
| **Objectives** | Research Search and Rescue and Wilderness First Responder work; apply a sling and a finger splint using the correct technique; take turns as responder and patient and reflect on what changed between rounds |
| **TEKS** | d(1)(C) |
| **5E Phases** | Engage: Warm-Up · Explore: SAR and WFR research · Explain: Sling and splint demonstration · Explore: Two responder rounds and role switch · Evaluate: Exit Ticket |
| **Deliverable** | Six research answers (p. 52) + a sling and finger splint applied in both responder rounds + scene notes started on the Patient Care Report |
| **Materials** | *Find Your Future* workbook pp. 52-53, Climber Notes deck "Injured on the Trail" (slides 2-3), one set of supplies per pair (triangular bandage or sling, cohesive wrap, finger splint), Chromebooks, projector |

---

## Warm-Up (5 min)

**WARM-UP: You are hiking two miles from the trailhead and your friend falls and hurts a shoulder. You cannot call 911 and get an ambulance to you. What do you do in the first five minutes?**

Take 3-4 responses. Students usually say "call for help" or "carry them out." Both raise the right problem: help is far away and moving an injured person the wrong way can make the injury worse. Bridge: that gap is a career. Today students work as the Wilderness First Responders who close it.

---

## Activity 1: Search and Rescue Research (15 min)

**Source:** (FYF p. 52: "Injured on the Trail", Step 1), a Career Climb activity

Open the workbook to page 52 and read the introduction together. Search and Rescue (SAR) teams respond to emergencies in remote places like mountains, forests, and deserts, using skill, strategy, and teamwork to reach people who need help. Inside SAR teams are specially trained medics called Wilderness First Responders (WFRs), who provide medical care in places where hospitals are far away. Their main goal is to stabilize the patient, which means keeping an injury from getting worse and protecting the patient until they can reach advanced care.

Then read the scene. While hiking down the steep and rocky Granite Ridge trail, a hiker slipped on loose gravel and fell hard. The hiker is sitting on the side of the trail, unable to continue, with a painful shoulder that looks out of place and a swollen, bent finger. Students and their partner are the first WFRs on the scene. The job is to assess the situation, stabilize the injuries, and prepare the hiker for safe transport.

**Step 1: Conduct Research.** Students pair up or join a small group and record answers to the workbook's six questions (p. 52):

1. Why is it important to stabilize a patient before transport?
2. Why is a splint necessary for a fractured finger?
3. How do SAR teams reach injured people in mountainous areas?
4. What could happen if injuries are not properly stabilized?
5. What is required to safely transport someone injured in the wilderness?
6. What are the proper techniques for applying a sling and finger splint?

Questions 1 through 5 are research questions students answer from the Hats & Ladders Hat pages, BLS, and a search. Question 6 is answered in Activity 2, so tell pairs to leave it blank and come back to it.

!!! tip "Facilitation Tip"
    Question 3 is the one that surfaces the career. Push pairs past "a helicopter" to the roles that make a rescue work: ground searchers, a technical rope team, an incident commander who runs the search grid, and the WFR who stays with the patient. Ask which of those jobs they would want and why.

## Activity 2: Sling and Splint Demonstration (10 min)

**Source:** (Climber Notes: "Injured on the Trail", slides 2-3)

Project the Climber Notes supply table and walk the three supplies students will use:

| Supply | What it is used for |
|---|---|
| Triangular bandage / sling | Supports the arm and limits shoulder movement |
| Cohesive wrap | Holds bandages or splints securely in place |
| Finger splint | Keeps the finger straight and prevents further injury |

**Chunking:** Teach one skill at a time. Demonstrate the sling first, using the Climber Notes photo of a triangular bandage supporting an arm, and say what the supply is doing before you show the steps. Close that chunk with a thumbs check: can every pair say what the sling is limiting and why that matters for a shoulder that looks out of place? Only then demonstrate the finger splint with the cohesive wrap, using the second Climber Notes photo, and close that chunk with a second thumbs check. Whichever skill draws the most thumbs down becomes a two minute re-teach at the start of Activity 3.

Distribute one supply set per pair at the end of the demonstration, not before it.

!!! warning "This is a simulation, not a certification"
    Students are practicing technique on a partner who is acting, not treating a real injury. Set two rules before supplies go out: nothing gets wrapped tightly enough that a finger cannot slide underneath, and any student with a real injury, a cast, or a brace is the note-taker for their pair rather than the patient. Real Wilderness First Responder certification is a multi-day course taken after high school.

## Activity 3: Work as a Wilderness First Responder (15 min)

**Source:** (FYF p. 53: "Injured on the Trail", Steps 2-3)

**Step 2: Work as a WFR (round 1, 6 min).** In each pair, one student is the WFR and one is the injured hiker on the Granite Ridge trail. The WFR assesses the scene, talks the patient through what is about to happen, applies the sling to the shoulder, then splints and wraps the finger. The workbook asks students to explain what they are doing as they work, so the patient is not guessing.

**Step 3: Reflect and Switch Roles (9 min).** Pairs stop and discuss what worked well, what was hard, and how their actions helped stabilize the patient. Then they switch roles and repeat the whole sequence. The second responder can use the first patient's feedback about a wrap that felt too tight or a sling that sat too low.

**Active Monitoring:** Walk a fixed pathway through the room twice, once per round. Round 1 target: the sling actually supports the arm and limits shoulder movement. Round 2 target: the finger splint keeps the finger straight and the cohesive wrap holds it without cutting off circulation. Carry a clipboard and note who is doing the work in each pair rather than watching. Have feedback ready for the two predictable errors: a wrap pulled so tight the fingertip changes color, and a sling knotted so low that the forearm hangs below the elbow. If more than a handful of pairs miss the round 1 target, reproject the Climber Notes photo to the whole room instead of fixing it pair by pair.

**DOK 3:** A storm is 10 minutes out and you only have time to stabilize ONE of the hiker's two injuries before you move. Which one do you choose, and what specifically makes that injury more urgent than the other?

---

## Activity 4: Start the Patient Care Report (3 min)

**Source:** (FYF p. 53: "Injured on the Trail", Step 4)

Students open the Patient Care Report on page 53 and fill in the first two sections only:

- **Scene Description:** Where was the patient, and what was the environment like?
- **Patient Condition:** What injuries did you observe, and what was the patient experiencing?

Write while the details are fresh. The remaining three sections are Day 4.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the six research questions with two of them pre-answered as models. Give a numbered picture card for the sling and for the splint so pairs can follow the steps without rereading prose. Pre-assign partners so students who struggle with negotiation can focus on the technique.
- **Extension:** Students who finish both rounds early research one more SAR role (rope technician, K-9 handler, incident commander, helicopter crew) and write two sentences on what that person does that a WFR does not.
- **ELL:** Bilingual supply cards. Pre-teach: Rescue = Rescate, Sling = Cabestrillo, Splint = Férula, Bandage = Venda, Shoulder = Hombro, Finger = Dedo, Injury = Lesión, Stabilize = Estabilizar. The Climber Notes technique photos carry the procedure visually, so ELL students can follow the demonstration without the text. Pair ESL students with bilingual peers for the research step.
