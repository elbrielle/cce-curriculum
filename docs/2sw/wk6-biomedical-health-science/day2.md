# Day 2: Mini Medics — Design a Medical Nanobot

## Lesson Overview

| | |
|---|---|
| **Time** | 50 minutes |
| **Objectives** | Complete the "Mini Medics" nanobot design activity: review the medical team's requirements, plan and size a nanobot, draw and label it on chart paper, map its journey through the body, and share the design with another team |
| **TEKS** | d(1)(C) |

## Warm-Up (5 min)

**WARM-UP: A doctor needs to reach a blood clot deep inside a patient's body. Name one way to get a tool or medicine to that exact spot without cutting the patient open.**

Take 3-4 responses (a pill, an IV line, a catheter, a scope). Bridge: engineers are now building machines so small they travel inside the bloodstream. Today the class designs one.

---

## Activity 1: Mini Medics — Mission Briefing (8 min)

**Source:** (FYF pp. 79-80: "Mini Medics"), a Career Climb activity

Open the workbook to page 79 and read the scenario together. Nanobots are tiny machines scientists are testing that could one day carry medicine to the exact place it is needed, break apart dangerous blockages, or help repair damaged tissue. Students are nanobot engineers, and their team has been asked to design a tiny medical robot that travels through the bloodstream to break up a dangerous blood clot before it blocks blood flow to the brain or heart.

Project the four constraints the medical team gives in Step 1 (p. 79):

- Blood is always moving and can push the robot in different directions
- Blood vessels are very narrow, so the nanobot must be extremely small
- The robot must break the clot apart without damaging the walls of the blood vessel
- Doctors guide the nanobot with magnetic tools and imaging machines that track its movement

Then project the four things the nanobot has to do: travel safely through the bloodstream, reach the clot, break the clot into smaller pieces so blood can flow again, and send a signal back to the doctors when the mission is complete.

Name the careers standing behind this one device: biomedical engineers design it, medical researchers test whether it is safe, and imaging technologists run the machines that track it inside the patient. All three sit in the Health Science cluster students have been exploring for four weeks.

!!! tip "Facilitation Tip"
    Post the four mission requirements where students can see them all period. Teams that skip the "send a signal back" requirement produce a robot that works and then disappears, which is the most common miss on this activity.

## Activity 2: Plan, Draw, and Label Your Nanobot (32 min)

**Source:** (FYF pp. 80-81: "Mini Medics", Steps 2-4)

Students work in pairs or teams of three. Run the workbook's three steps one at a time and check each before releasing the next.

**Step 2: Plan Your Nanobot (10 min).** Teams fill in the workbook's plan on p. 80:

- **Name of your nanobot**
- **How big is it?** Compare its size to something familiar so other people can picture how small it is
- **How does your nanobot work with the tools doctors use to guide it?**
- **What equipment helps doctors track or steer your robot?**
- **What special tools does your nanobot have to break up the blood clot?** Three tools, listed separately

Check before moving on: every team has a size comparison and three named tools.

**Step 3: Draw and Label Your Nanobot (14 min).** Teams draw their nanobot large on chart paper or poster board. Every important part and tool gets a label, and each label carries a short note saying what that part does and how it helps complete the mission.

**Time, Voice, Body:** Voice 1 for team drawing talk and Voice 0 for the last two minutes of labeling so teams can hear their own thinking. Markers stay capped on the table between uses, chart paper stays flat on the desk rather than on the floor, and a visible 14 minute countdown runs on the screen.

Check before moving on: every part on the drawing has a label, and every label has a purpose written next to it.

**Step 4: Plan Your Nanobot's Journey (8 min).** Teams write or sketch how the nanobot completes the mission inside the body, answering the workbook's four questions: how does the nanobot enter the patient's body, how does it travel through the bloodstream, what happens when it reaches the blood clot, and what happens after the mission is complete.

**DOK 3:** Two teams built nanobots the same size but chose different tools to break the clot. What evidence would a medical researcher need before deciding which design is safer for a real patient?

!!! tip "Facilitation Tip"
    Watch for size comparisons like "very tiny" or "super small." Those are not comparisons. Push for something a reader can picture: smaller than a grain of salt, about the width of a human hair, one tenth the size of a red blood cell. The comparison is what makes the rest of the design believable.

---

## Activity 3: Present Your Nanobot (3 min)

**Source:** (FYF p. 81: "Mini Medics", Step 5)

Each team joins one other team. Both share their nanobot design and explain how it completes the mission, naming the tools and ideas each person contributed. The joined group then decides which of the two designs is the most creative, the most useful, or the most likely to work in the real world, and that design is the one nominated to present to the whole class.

If time allows, take the workbook's class discussion question (p. 81): if nanobots become common in medicine, what new jobs might exist to design, control, or repair them?

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a pre-drawn nanobot outline students label rather than drawing from scratch. Provide a size-comparison word bank (grain of salt, human hair, grain of sand, red blood cell, dust speck) and a tool word bank (spinning blade, heat tip, medicine sprayer, tiny drill, magnet arm, suction tube).
- **Extension:** Take the workbook's second class discussion question and design around it: what challenges might engineers face when building robots that travel through the human body? Add one safety feature to the drawing that answers a challenge you named, and label what it does.
- **ELL:** Bilingual anatomy and design glossary (Blood clot = Coágulo, Blood vessel = Vaso sanguíneo, Bloodstream = Torrente sanguíneo, Tool = Herramienta, Signal = Señal, Label = Etiqueta). Allow labels to be written in Spanish. The drawing carries most of the meaning, so students can present the design visually and name parts in either language.
