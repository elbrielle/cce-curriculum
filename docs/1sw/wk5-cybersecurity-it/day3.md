# Day 3: Design a Canva Cybersecurity Bootcamp Flyer
<!-- CCE DAILY CONTRACT START -->
## Daily Learning Contract

- **Topic:** Work Ethic and Integrity
- **Objective:** Students will identify work ethic and integrity in a cybersecurity task by planning a community lesson, checking advice, and beginning a privacy-safe Canva flyer.
- **TEKS:** d(4)(F)
- **Demonstration of Learning:** FYF p. 35 holds the six planning answers, or the packet holds one alternate plan for a student who needs it. A one-page Canva flyer draft shows an audience, two accurate safety actions, event details, and a safe sign-up method. The final flyer is the one Major 2 submission on Day 5.

## Before Students Arrive

1. Open FYF pp. 34-35 and the completed teacher model. Project the packet's flyer checklist as clear design criteria. FYF p. 35 is the default place to plan; give the packet's planning spaces only as an alternative scaffold, never as a second completed plan.
2. Confirm students can open Canva with their school accounts. Project the path to a portrait flyer design and show how to replace sample text and images.
3. Open the Canvas flyer assignment and rubric. Students can view it on Day 3; final upload is planned for Day 5.
4. Prepare one fictional sign-up example, such as “Ask your teacher” or “Sign up at the library desk.” Do not use student contact information or post real flyers.

## Do Now: What happens before a safe lesson? (5 min)

Define **work ethic** as doing careful work and following through, and **integrity** as acting honestly even when no one sees the choice. Ask, “Before you teach someone how to spot a scam, what would you check?” Take two workplace examples, such as an analyst checking a safety tip, testing a demonstration, or removing private information. Ask students which example shows work ethic and which shows integrity, and listen for an action and a reason.

## Activity 1: Read the need and set the boundaries (8 min)

Open FYF p. 34. Students choose one audience and one topic from the printed options. The percentages on that page belong to the workbook scenario; students do not repeat them on the flyer as current statistics. Ask, “Would you trust a flyer that promises its advice stops every scam?” Expected: no. Model one specific, cautious action, such as opening a known app instead of a link in a suspicious message.

## Activity 2: Plan once in FYF (12 min)

Open FYF p. 35. Students answer the six planning questions in the workbook. Model how the two learning goals become actions participants can practice: “Pause before opening a suspicious link” and “Check the sender through a known contact.” Circulate at minute 18 to check for one audience and two actions. At minute 23, ask, “Does your activity let people practice what you promised to teach?” Students repair the plan before opening Canva.

Use the class discussion prompt on p. 35 to name one behind-the-scenes work-ethic action and one integrity choice. A specific answer names a check and a reason: “We tested the advice and removed personal contact details so participants could rely on the flyer.” Students may discuss this with a partner; no second written response is required.

## Activity 3: Build the Canva draft (20 min)

Students open Canva with their school accounts, search for a portrait flyer design, and replace the sample content. Show the title, audience, topic, time/place, and teacher-approved sign-up path on a model. Require two specific online-safety actions. Teach students to enlarge the title, use readable contrast, and select one relevant visual. They write original text and credit reused material. The teacher checks at minute 34 that the safety advice is accurate and at minute 42 that no personal phone number, email, or home location appears.

Use the packet's **Flyer Checklist** as a design check while students build. Its seven criteria and safe sign-up rule make the workbook's open-ended flyer prompt concrete; students do not submit the checklist as another assignment.

A template is a starting layout, not permission to keep unrelated sample claims or graphics. If Canva is inaccessible for an individual student, the teacher selects an accessible design format and scores the same flyer evidence. The whole-class task remains Canva.

## Close: One useful revision (5 min)

Partners identify one clear element and one change that would help the audience read or trust the flyer. Students save their Canva drafts and name one revision they will make on Day 5. The final PNG or PDF goes into the linked Canvas assignment on Day 5. Do not collect the workbook plan again through Canvas.

## Teacher Key and Misconceptions

- Work ethic appears in preparation, checking facts, revising, and finishing the design carefully. Integrity appears in original or credited content, accurate advice, and privacy-safe sign-up information.
- Two actions are stronger than a broad slogan. “Be careful online” does not tell an audience what to do.
- A Canva template may contain placeholder facts. Students must replace them with their own event information and supported advice.
- The flyer is a fictional classroom design. No student contacts real participants or publishes the event.

## Supports and Recovery

- Offer the words **audience, action, sign-up, check, credit** beside FYF p. 35. Rehearse: “Our audience can ___ to stay safer because ___.”
- Read the six workbook questions aloud and allow oral planning before students write.
- If a student needs more planning structure, offer the existing bootcamp packet as their one planning surface. Its flyer checklist is available to everyone, whether they plan in FYF or the packet.
- For an absent student, FYF pp. 34-35 and the Canvas Student Guide provide the same plan and Canva task. A teacher-approved accessible design format replaces Canva only when needed; it is not a second submission.
