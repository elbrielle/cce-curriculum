## Why this lesson is structured this way

Students are not deciding whether a career is universally “good” or “bad.” They are learning that the same schedule, work setting, duty, or training requirement can feel different to different people. The lesson moves from a familiar example to a complete model, guided practice, and independent analysis so students can explain a classification with evidence instead of writing a vague opinion.

## Before students arrive

- Place one *Find Your Future* workbook at each seat, open to pp. 4–5.
- Open the Day 5 deck to the Do Now.
- Choose one approved career-information route students already know: a Hats & Ladders career card, an FYF career profile, or a teacher-provided career profile.
- Put the word bank where students can see it: **perk, neutral, quirk, duty, schedule, work setting, training, work value, preference**.
- Keep blank five-column paper tables available for students without a workbook. Paper and FYF are equivalent routes; students complete one route only.

## Complete model and non-model

### Complete model

> **Career:** Veterinary Technician
> **Detail:** May work evening or weekend emergency shifts.
> **Mark:** Quirk
> **Why:** I prefer a predictable daytime schedule, so changing hours would be difficult for me.
> **Source:** Hats & Ladders career profile.

### Non-model

> **Career:** Vet
> **Detail:** Good job.
> **Mark:** Perk
> **Why:** I like it.

Ask, “What information is missing?” Students should notice that the non-model has no specific duty or work condition, no connection to a work value, and no usable source.

## Fifty-minute facilitator flow

### 1. Welcome and Do Now · 5 minutes

Welcome students to class and direct them to open FYF to p. 4. Project: **“A job requires working outside during a Texas summer. Is that a perk, neutral, or quirk for you? Why?”**

- Give students one quiet minute to decide.
- Ask for a thumbs-up, flat hand, or thumbs-down response.
- Invite two students with different answers to explain.
- Say, “The work condition stayed the same. Our preferences changed the classification. Today you will use that idea to analyze one career.”

### 2. Teach the three classifications · 10 minutes

Use the FYF p. 4 Pest Control Technician example.

- Define **perk** as a job detail that fits what a person values or prefers.
- Define **neutral** as a detail that neither attracts nor discourages the person.
- Define **quirk** as a demanding or unusual detail that may not fit the person’s preferences.
- Point to one duty, one schedule detail, and one work-setting detail.
- Ask, “Could two people mark this detail differently? What personal value would explain each answer?”
- Emphasize that a quirk is not automatically a reason to reject a career. It is a tradeoff to understand.

### 3. Guided model and oral rehearsal · 10 minutes

Display the Veterinary Technician model.

- Read the specific career detail aloud.
- Think aloud: “I notice the changing schedule. I value predictable hours. That evidence makes this a quirk for me.”
- Contrast it with: “Someone who values variety might call the same schedule a perk.”
- Show the response pattern: **specific fact → P/N/Q mark → personal value or preference → source**.
- Ask partners to rehearse one response using: **“I marked ___ as a ___ because I value/prefer ___.”**
- Call on one partner to share while the other points to the career fact that supports the answer.

### 4. Independent career analysis · 20 minutes

Students complete the first career table on FYF p. 5.

- Students choose one career from an FYF profile, a prior Hats & Ladders recommendation, or a teacher-provided profile.
- Students record at least four specific details across duties, schedule, work setting, training, or tools.
- Students mark each detail as a perk, neutral, or quirk for themselves.
- Students explain each mark with a personal work value or preference.
- Students name the source once in the table.

At minute 30, scan for specific facts rather than labels such as “fun,” “easy,” or “good money.” Ask, “What does the worker actually do, use, study, or experience?” At minute 40, ask students to star their strongest row and rehearse it with a partner.

### 5. Demonstration of Learning and cleanup · 5 minutes

Ask students to check three things:

1. I recorded at least four specific career details.
2. Every detail has a P/N/Q mark and a reason connected to me.
3. I named the source I used.

Invite one student to complete the sentence: **“A career detail is not automatically a perk or a quirk because…”** Students return workbooks to the class shelf and leave the table open to p. 5 for a quick teacher scan. There is no second upload or copied response.

## Strategic language and access supports

- **Focused word bank:** perk, neutral, quirk, duty, schedule, work setting, training, work value, preference.
- **Visual cue:** thumbs up = perk, flat hand = neutral, thumbs down = quirk.
- **Oral rehearsal:** partners say a complete reason before writing it.
- **Optional sentence stem:** “I marked ___ as a ___ because I value/prefer ___.”
- **Clarify essential vocabulary:** teachers may pair home-language equivalents for the three classification words when helpful; do not translate every direction automatically.
- **Extension:** Analyze a second career with a very different work setting and compare one tradeoff.

## Recovery routes

- **Workbook unavailable:** Use the equivalent five-column paper table: Career Detail | P/N/Q | Why | Work Value/Preference | Source.
- **Career source unavailable:** Use the teacher-provided profile and complete the same thinking target.
- **Absent student:** Read the FYF p. 4 model and complete one p. 5 table; no Xello catch-up is attached to this lesson.
